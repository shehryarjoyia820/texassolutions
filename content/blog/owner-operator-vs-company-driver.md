The choice between **owner-operator vs company driver** is one of the biggest decisions a truck driver makes. Running your own truck promises more money and more control, but it also turns you into a business owner with real costs and real risk. This guide compares both paths honestly so you can pick the one that fits your finances, skills and goals.

## The basic difference

- **Company driver:** you drive the carrier's truck, the carrier pays all operating costs, and you get a paycheck, usually per mile, per hour, per load or a percentage. You are typically a W-2 employee.
- **Owner-operator:** you own or lease the truck and pay for fuel, insurance, maintenance, tolls and more. You earn the revenue from loads, and what is left after costs is your income. You are self-employed.

Owner-operators either **lease on** to a carrier and run under its authority, or run under **their own authority** (their own MC number) and work directly with brokers and shippers.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Factor | Company driver | Owner-operator |
|---|---|---|
| Truck cost | None | Payment or lease, plus down payment |
| Operating costs | Paid by carrier | Paid by you |
| Income | Predictable paycheck | Varies with rates, miles and costs |
| Benefits | Often health, retirement, paid time off | You buy your own |
| Taxes | Withheld by employer | Estimated taxes and self-employment tax |
| Choice of loads | Usually assigned | You choose (or approve) loads |
| Home time | Set by carrier policy | You decide, but it affects income |
| Risk | Low financial risk | Breakdowns and slow markets hit you directly |
| Paperwork | Minimal | Bookkeeping, IFTA, permits and more |

## What owner-operators really earn

The big gross numbers can be misleading. Here is a hypothetical week to show the difference between revenue and income.

- Revenue: 2,500 miles x $2.40 = **$6,000**
- Fuel (2,500 miles / 6.5 mpg x $3.90): about $1,500
- Truck payment: $600
- Insurance: $350
- Maintenance and tire reserve: $400
- Dispatch, ELD, permits and other costs: $500
- **Total costs: about $3,350**
- **Left before taxes: about $2,650**

From that, the owner-operator still pays income and self-employment taxes, health insurance and saves for slow weeks. A company driver earning, say, $1,500 to $1,800 a week in this hypothetical has fewer worries, but less upside. ## Advantages of each path

### Company driver

- Steady, predictable pay
- No truck payment or repair bills
- Benefits may be included
- Less paperwork and fewer business decisions
- Easier to change jobs if a company is a bad fit
- Time to learn the industry before taking on risk

### Owner-operator

- Higher earning potential when rates and costs are managed well
- Control over which loads you take and where you run
- Ability to build equity in a truck and a business
- Business expenses may be deductible (ask a CPA)
- Option to grow into a small fleet

## The risks of going independent

Owner-operators fail most often for reasons they could have planned for:

- **Not knowing cost per mile** and taking loads that lose money
- **Too little cash reserve** for a major repair or a slow month
- **Overpriced trucks** with payments the business cannot support
- **Waiting to get paid** for 30 days or more without a plan for cash flow
- **Underestimating paperwork** like IFTA, 2290 and compliance

None of these are reasons not to do it. They are reasons to prepare.

## Signs you are ready to become an owner-operator

Use this checklist before you make the move:

- [ ] At least a couple of years of safe driving experience
- [ ] A clean driving record and inspection history
- [ ] Savings for a down payment and several months of costs
- [ ] A written budget with your expected cost per mile
- [ ] A plan for freight: lease on, own authority with a dispatcher, or direct customers
- [ ] Basic bookkeeping skills or a bookkeeper lined up
- [ ] A clear understanding of the insurance you will need

If several boxes are unchecked, staying a company driver for another year while you save and learn can be the smarter business decision. Our guide on [how to become an owner-operator](/blog/how-to-become-an-owner-operator.html) covers the steps in detail.

## Lease on or get your own authority?

Leasing on to a carrier is a middle step: you own the truck, but the carrier provides authority, insurance options and often freight. It is simpler but you give up some control and pay the carrier a share. Getting your own authority gives you full control but means more setup and compliance. Some new carriers also explore [MC lease options](/mc-lease.html) as they build their own track record. Talk to an attorney about any lease agreement before you sign.
