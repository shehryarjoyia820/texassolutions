The two jobs sound similar because both involve finding freight, but they work for **opposite sides** of the deal. Knowing the difference protects your rate and your authority.

## Side-by-side

| | Truck dispatcher | Freight broker |
|---|---|---|
| Works for | The carrier (you) | The shipper, and their own margin |
| Paid by | The carrier, often a percentage of weekly gross | Shipper pays the broker; broker pays the carrier less a margin |
| Needs FMCSA authority | No, uses the carrier's MC | Yes, broker authority |
| Financial requirement | None from FMCSA | Surety bond or trust of $75,000 |
| Main job | Find, negotiate and book loads for your truck | Match shipper freight to available carriers |
| Legal responsibility for freight | Carrier's authority is on the load | Contracts with the shipper |

## What a dispatcher actually does

- Searches load boards and broker networks for freight that fits your equipment and lanes
- Negotiates the rate before the load reaches you
- Plans your next load to cut deadhead miles
- Completes broker setup packets and keeps rate confirmations organized

You approve every load. Nothing is booked under your authority without your say-so.

## What a broker actually does

A broker is a middleman: the shipper hires them to find a truck, and they hire you. Their profit is the gap between what the shipper pays and what you are paid, so their interest in your rate is different from yours. Good brokers build long relationships; that is not the same as being on your side of a negotiation.

## Why the difference matters

- **Rate negotiation:** A dispatcher's income rises when your gross rises. A broker's margin can shrink if you negotiate higher.
- **Paperwork:** A dispatcher books under *your* authority, so your MC, insurance and W-9 go on the rate confirmation.
- **Trust:** Check any broker on FMCSA before you haul: active authority, bond on file, and payment history.
