Breakdowns cost far more than the repair bill. A truck in the shop earns nothing, can miss appointments, and may leave you paying for a hotel and a tow. A written **truck maintenance schedule** turns surprise repairs into planned, cheaper ones and helps keep you clear of roadside violations.

## Why a schedule beats fixing things when they break

Preventive maintenance is about catching small problems early. A worn belt, a slow air leak or a weeping hub seal is cheap to fix in your own time. The same problem on the side of the highway can cost a load, a tow and a day or more of revenue.

A schedule also helps with:

- **Roadside inspections:** fewer defects mean fewer violations and out-of-service orders.
- **Safety scores:** vehicle maintenance violations can affect your carrier's safety record.
- **Resale value:** a truck with full service records is easier to sell.
- **Budgeting:** you know roughly what is due and when.

## Federal rules to know

FMCSA's rules on inspection, repair and maintenance are in 49 CFR Part 396. In plain terms, as generally understood:

- Every commercial motor vehicle must be systematically inspected, repaired and maintained.
- Drivers must be satisfied the vehicle is in safe operating condition before driving it (the pre-trip inspection).
- Drivers must complete a written post-trip inspection report (DVIR) when required, and defects that affect safety must be repaired before the vehicle runs again.
- Each vehicle needs a periodic inspection at least once every 12 months, done by a qualified inspector, with proof kept in the vehicle or available.
- Maintenance records must be kept for a set period.

Details, exceptions and record retention periods matter, so confirm current requirements in 49 CFR Part 396 on the FMCSA website or with a compliance professional.

## The schedule, interval by interval

### Daily checks

Every day you drive:

- Pre-trip inspection: tires, wheels and lugs, brakes and air system, lights and reflectors, coupling devices, steering, mirrors, horn, wipers, fluid leaks.
- Check engine oil, coolant and DEF levels.
- Drain air tanks if your system does not have automatic drains.
- Post-trip inspection and DVIR, noting any defects.

### Weekly and monthly checks

| Interval | What to check |
|---|---|
| Weekly | Tire pressure with a gauge, tread depth, battery terminals, belts and hoses, fifth wheel lubrication and wear, trailer lights and seals |
| Monthly | Brake adjustment and lining wear, suspension and air bags, exhaust and aftertreatment for leaks, wiper blades, cab and sleeper HVAC, fire extinguisher and warning triangles |

These are good habits, not a substitute for your manufacturers' recommendations.

### Preventive maintenance (PM) services

PM services happen at mileage or engine-hour intervals set by your engine, transmission and component manufacturers. Intervals depend on the make, model, oil type and how hard the truck works, so use your owner's manuals as the source.

A typical PM service may include:

1. Engine oil and filter change.
2. Fuel filters and water separator.
3. Air filter check or replacement.
4. Chassis lubrication (grease all fittings).
5. Coolant condition and level check.
6. Brake, steering and suspension inspection.
7. Driveline, U-joints and wheel-end inspection.
8. Battery and charging system test.
9. Review of fault codes and aftertreatment health.

Less frequent items, such as transmission and differential fluids, valve adjustments, coolant replacement and aftertreatment cleaning, come up at longer intervals. Track them on the same schedule so they do not slip.

**Consider oil analysis.**

Sending an oil sample to a lab can reveal coolant leaks, fuel dilution or unusual wear before they become failures. It can also help you decide whether an extended drain interval is safe for your engine.

### Annual and seasonal items

- **Annual periodic inspection:** schedule it before it is due, not on the last day.
- **Before winter:** batteries, coolant protection, block heater, wipers, fuel water separator, chains.
- **Before summer:** cooling system, A/C, belts and hoses; for reefers, the reefer unit service.
- **Trailer:** brakes, lights, tires, doors and seals, landing gear. Reefer units have their own service schedule.

## Keep records

Good records help with compliance, roadside questions and resale. For each service, write down:

- Date, mileage and engine hours.
- Work done and parts used.
- Who did the work.
- Cost.

A simple spreadsheet or maintenance app works, as long as you use it every time.

## Budget for maintenance

Repairs come in lumps, but you can save for them in small, steady amounts. A common approach is to put a set amount per mile into a separate maintenance account.

### Worked example (hypothetical)

Say you choose to set aside $0.18 per mile and drive 9,000 miles a month:

- 9,000 x $0.18 = **$1,620 per month**
- Over 12 months: about **$19,440** for tires, PM services and repairs

An older truck may need more; a newer truck under warranty may need less. Use the [maintenance budget calculator](/maintenance-budget-calculator.html) to set your number, and include it in your [cost per mile](/cost-per-mile-calculator.html) so your rates cover it.

## Maintenance checklist summary

- [ ] Pre-trip and post-trip inspections every driving day.
- [ ] Weekly tire, belt, battery and fifth wheel checks.
- [ ] Monthly brake, suspension and exhaust checks.
- [ ] PM services at manufacturer intervals.
- [ ] Annual periodic inspection scheduled ahead of time.
- [ ] Seasonal prep before winter and summer.
- [ ] Every service recorded with date, mileage and cost.
- [ ] Money set aside per mile in a maintenance account.
