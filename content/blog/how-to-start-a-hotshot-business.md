Hotshot trucking uses a heavy-duty pickup and a flatbed trailer to move loads that are too small, too urgent or too oddly shaped for a full semi load. Think equipment, machinery parts, building materials, pipe and other freight that needs to move now.

It costs less to start than a semi operation, but it is not cheap, and the weight rules are easy to get wrong. Here is how to set one up step by step.

## Step 1: Understand what hotshot freight looks like

Hotshot loads are usually:

- **Partial or less-than-truckload** in size, often well under what a 48 ft flatbed carries.
- **Time-sensitive.** A broken-down machine or a jobsite waiting on material pays for speed.
- **Open-deck.** Loaded by forklift or crane from the side or top, so securement skills matter.

Rates often run about **$4 to $5 a mile**, depending on lane, urgency and season. See our post on [hotshot rates per mile and fuel](/blog/hotshot-trucking-rates-per-mile-fuel-and-dispatch.html) for more on what moves the number. Rates are never guaranteed.

## Step 2: Choose your truck and trailer

The common setup is a **1-ton diesel dually pulling a 40 ft gooseneck**. When you shop, focus on weight ratings first and price second.

**Truck checklist:**

- GVWR and GCWR (gross combination weight rating) from the manufacturer's label and specs.
- Towing capacity for a gooseneck or fifth-wheel hitch.
- Diesel engine condition and service records.

**Trailer checklist:**

- Deck length (30, 35 and 40 ft are common).
- Trailer GVWR and axle ratings.
- Ramps, winches, stake pockets and tie-down points.
- Empty weight, since every pound of trailer is a pound you cannot haul.

## Step 3: Decide CDL or non-CDL

This decision shapes the whole business.

Under federal rules, a combination generally requires a **Class A CDL** when its gross combination weight rating or actual combination weight (whichever is greater) is **26,001 lbs or more** and the towed unit is rated over **10,000 lbs**. Many 1-ton trucks paired with a heavy-duty gooseneck land in Class A territory.

Some operators run a lighter, "non-CDL" setup by keeping the combination at 26,000 lbs or less. That avoids the CDL but sharply limits payload. Our guide to [non-CDL box truck and hotshot loads](/blog/non-cdl-box-truck-and-hotshot-loads.html) explains how to check this carefully. Always confirm with FMCSA and your state licensing agency before you buy equipment.

If you hold a CDL, drug and alcohol testing rules, including the FMCSA Clearinghouse, apply to you.

## Step 4: Set up the company and authority

The paperwork is similar to any for-hire trucking company:

- Form a business entity, get an EIN and open a business bank account.
- Get a **USDOT number** and **interstate operating authority** (MC number) from FMCSA.
- File a **BOC-3** for process agents.
- Register for **UCR** each year.
- Check whether **IRP** apportioned plates and **IFTA** apply. Combinations over 26,000 lbs commonly qualify, but confirm with your base state.

Our guides on [getting an MC number](/blog/how-to-get-mc-number-authority-owner-operator.html) and [filing IFTA](/blog/ifta-for-owner-operators-how-to-file.html) go deeper. ## Step 5: Get insured

FMCSA sets the minimum liability you must have on file. Brokers then set their own requirements, and many want higher auto liability and cargo coverage. Hotshot insurance can be expensive for new authorities, so get several quotes before you commit to equipment.

## Step 6: Gear up for securement

Open-deck freight means you are responsible for securing it properly. Plan for:

- Ratchet straps, chains and binders rated for the loads you will carry.
- Edge protectors and corner guards.
- Tarps if you plan to haul freight that must stay dry.
- Know the cargo securement rules, including how many tie-downs a load needs.

A shifted load can cost you a claim, a ticket and a broker relationship in one trip.

## Step 7: Run the numbers on a sample week

Here is a rough example using assumed figures. Your numbers will differ.

**Assumptions:**

- Weekly gross: $7,500
- Total miles (loaded plus empty): 2,000
- Average fuel economy across loaded and empty miles: 11 mpg
- Diesel price: $4.50 a gallon
- Dispatch at 7% of gross
- Insurance: $400 a week
- Truck and trailer payments: $600 a week
- Maintenance and tire reserve: $0.20 a mile

**Math:**

- Fuel: 2,000 / 11 = 181.8 gallons x $4.50 = **$818**
- Dispatch: $7,500 x 0.07 = **$525**
- Maintenance reserve: 2,000 x $0.20 = **$400**
- Fixed costs: $400 + $600 = **$1,000**
- Total costs: $818 + $525 + $400 + $1,000 = **$2,743**
- Left before taxes, factoring, permits and other expenses: $7,500 - $2,743 = **$4,757**

Slow weeks, repairs and time off will pull the average down, so plan around a range, not one good week. For more detail see [how much hotshot drivers make](/blog/how-much-do-hotshot-drivers-make.html), and check your own fuel with the [truck fuel cost calculator](/truck-fuel-cost-calculator.html).

## Step 8: Find loads and decide on a dispatcher

You can find hotshot freight on load boards, through direct broker relationships, or with a hotshot dispatch company. Our guide to the [best load boards for hotshot](/blog/best-load-boards-for-hotshot.html) covers the first option.

A dispatcher for hotshot trucks is useful when:

- You are new and do not have broker contacts yet.
- You lose hours each day searching boards and making calls.
- You want someone to handle broker packets, rate confirmations and invoicing.
- You want help planning reloads so you are not stuck empty after a delivery.

Before you sign with anyone, read [how to choose a truck dispatcher and avoid scams](/blog/how-to-choose-a-truck-dispatcher-avoid-scams.html).
