Load boards are where most box truck owner-operators find their first loads, and many keep using them for years. The challenge is that box truck freight is a smaller slice of what gets posted, so the way you search matters as much as which board you pay for.

This guide covers how to pick a box truck load board, how to set up searches that actually return usable freight, and what to do when the boards run dry. For a general comparison across all equipment types, see our guide to the [best load boards for owner-operators](/blog/best-load-boards-for-owner-operators.html).

## What makes a load board good for box trucks

When you compare boards, look past the total load count. A board with huge numbers of 53 ft van loads does not help a 26 ft box truck. Focus on:

- **Box truck and straight truck volume in your lanes.** The question is how many loads you can actually haul, not how many exist.
- **Equipment filters.** You need to filter for straight or box trucks, and ideally by length, weight and features like liftgate.
- **Broker information.** Credit scores, days-to-pay and contact details help you avoid slow payers.
- **Rate data.** Seeing recent lane rates helps you negotiate.
- **Mobile app quality.** You will search from the cab, so it needs to work well on a phone.
- **Cost against bookings.** A subscription is worth it only if it pays for itself in booked loads.

## Well-known load boards to compare

These are public, widely used boards. We are naming them so you know where to start, not endorsing any of them, and plans and features change, so check each site directly.

### DAT

One of the largest load boards in the US. Offers equipment filters, broker information and rate tools depending on the plan.

### Truckstop

Another large board with load search, broker credit information and rate tools depending on plan level.

### 123Loadboard

A board that many smaller carriers use, with load search and broker information features depending on plan.

### Broker carrier portals

Many larger brokers post loads on their own websites or apps for approved carriers. Once you are set up with a broker, their portal can be a steady source of freight.

## How to find box truck loads on a board

### Set your search up correctly

- **Equipment:** straight truck or box truck. Also try small van or partial loads that fit your truck.
- **Origin:** your current city, with a deadhead radius you can live with. Start around 50 to 100 miles and widen it if needed.
- **Destination:** leave it open, or set states where you know reloads are good.
- **Weight and length:** set to what your truck can legally haul, using your payload, not your GVWR.

### Move fast, but check first

Good box truck loads go quickly. Before you call:

- Check the broker's credit and days-to-pay.
- Confirm the load fits: pallet count, weight, liftgate needs, dock or no dock.
- Ask about appointment times, detention terms and lumper charges.

### Check the numbers before you say yes

Example with assumed figures:

- Load pays **$700** for 280 loaded miles, with 60 empty miles to pick up
- Your all-in cost per mile (assumption): **$1.45**
- Cost: (280 + 60) x $1.45 = **$493**
- Profit: $700 - $493 = **$207**, before any dispatch or factoring fees
- Effective rate per mile driven: $700 / 340 = **$2.06**

A load that looks like $2.50 a mile ($700 / 280) is closer to $2.06 once the empty miles count. Run your own numbers with the [load profitability calculator](/load-profitability-calculator.html), and figure out your real cost with the [cost per mile calculator](/cost-per-mile-calculator.html).

## Box truck loads near me: local search tips

If you want to stay close to home:

- Search by your home city with a small radius, then sort by posting time.
- Note which brokers post in your area again and again, and call them directly.
- Look for regional loads that let you get home most nights, and accept that they may pay less per load but more per mile.
- Ask brokers you deliver for if they have anything going back toward your home base.

## When load boards are not enough

Boards are competitive, and new box truck carriers often see the same thing: good loads disappear before they can call, or brokers will not set up a new MC. A few other ways to find box truck loads:

- **Direct broker relationships.** After a few clean loads, ask the broker for more freight in the same lanes.
- **Shippers and local businesses.** Some freight never hits a board. We cover this in [box truck contracts and how to get them](/blog/box-truck-contracts-how-to-get-them.html).
- **A dispatcher.** A dispatcher searches the boards, calls brokers, negotiates and handles setup packets for you.

Our post on [how to find loads for your truck](/blog/how-to-find-loads-for-your-truck.html) covers more of these options.

## Should you pay for a board or a dispatcher?

They solve different problems.

- **A load board** gives you access to postings. You still do the searching, calling, negotiating and paperwork.
- **A dispatcher** does that work for you and usually uses boards as one of several tools.

Some owner-operators start on their own with a board, then bring in a dispatcher once they are spending more time on the phone than driving. Others start with a dispatcher because their authority is new and they have no broker contacts yet. Either way, read [how to choose a truck dispatcher and avoid scams](/blog/how-to-choose-a-truck-dispatcher-avoid-scams.html) before signing anything.
