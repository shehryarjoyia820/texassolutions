Choosing between a sole proprietorship and a **trucking LLC** is one of the first business decisions a new owner-operator makes, and good bookkeeping is what keeps that business alive afterward. This guide explains the common options in plain English and gives you a simple weekly routine for your books. It is general education; confirm your entity choice with an attorney and your tax setup with a CPA.

## Sole proprietor, LLC or corporation

Here is how the common structures generally compare:

| Structure | How it generally works | Things to weigh |
|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | You and the business are the same for legal and tax purposes | Simplest to start; no separation between business and personal liability |
| Single-member LLC | A separate legal entity under state law; by default the IRS treats it like a sole proprietor for income tax | State filing fees and annual reports; separation only works if you keep money separate |
| LLC taxed as an S corporation | An LLC that elects S corp tax treatment; owner may take a salary plus distributions | Payroll, extra filings and accounting costs; only sensible at certain profit levels |
| Corporation | Separate entity with its own formalities | More paperwork; rarely the first choice for a one-truck operator |

Two points matter most:

1. **An LLC is not insurance.** Your auto liability and cargo coverage are your real protection against claims. An LLC's separation can be lost if you treat business money as personal money.
2. **Tax treatment is a separate choice.** An LLC can be taxed in different ways. Whether an S corp election makes sense depends on your profit and costs, so have a CPA run the numbers.

## Setting up the business the right way

If you choose an LLC, the usual order looks like this:

1. **Form the LLC with your state** and pick a registered agent.
2. **Get an EIN** from the IRS (free).
3. **Open a business checking account** in the LLC's name.
4. **Apply for your USDOT and MC number** under the business name. Use the exact business name you registered.
5. **Get insurance** in the business name, matching your authority.
6. **Set up your books** before your first load, not after tax season starts.

Make sure the legal name is exactly the same on your LLC filing, EIN letter, bank account, FMCSA records and insurance. Mismatches slow down broker setup and factoring approvals. For the full startup path, read [how to start a trucking company](/blog/how-to-start-a-trucking-company.html).

## Keep business and personal money apart

This is the single most useful bookkeeping habit:

- All load payments go into the business account.
- All business expenses come out of the business account or a business card.
- You pay yourself with a transfer to your personal account (an owner's draw or salary, depending on structure).
- Personal expenses never touch the business account.

Clean separation makes your books faster, your deductions easier to prove and your LLC's separation more meaningful.

## A simple chart of accounts for trucking

You do not need hundreds of categories. A one-truck operation can start with these:

**Income**
- Linehaul revenue
- Fuel surcharge
- Accessorials (detention, layover, TONU, lumper reimbursements)

**Expenses**
- Fuel and DEF
- Repairs and maintenance
- Tires
- Insurance
- Truck and trailer payments (split into interest and principal with your CPA's help)
- Dispatch fees
- Factoring fees
- Permits, licenses and registration
- Tolls and scales
- ELD, phone and software
- Office, accounting and legal
- Travel and per diem days (tracked as your CPA advises)

Categories that match how you run the truck make it easy to compare against your [cost per mile calculator](/cost-per-mile-calculator.html) results.

## The 30-minute weekly bookkeeping routine

Pick one day a week and do this every time:

1. **Record each load:** date, broker, origin, destination, loaded miles, empty miles, rate and accessorials.
2. **Match payments:** mark which invoices were paid and which are still open.
3. **Enter expenses:** upload fuel receipts, repair invoices and fees. Photograph paper receipts the same day you get them.
4. **Move tax money:** transfer your set percentage into a separate tax account.
5. **Check the balance:** confirm the bank balance roughly matches what your books say.

Once a month, reconcile every account line by line and review your profit. Once a quarter, pull your miles and gallons for IFTA and send your numbers to your CPA for estimated taxes. Our [trucking tax basics](/blog/trucking-tax-basics.html) guide explains why those quarterly steps matter.

## Worked example: why weekly books catch problems

Imagine a **hypothetical** owner-operator who reviews books weekly. In week three the numbers show:

- Revenue booked: $6,000
- Payments received: $2,400
- Open invoices: $3,600, one of them 40 days old

Without weekly books, that 40-day invoice might go unnoticed until rent is due. With them, the owner calls the broker the same day. Unpaid invoices are a cash flow problem even when the business is profitable on paper. If you factor, check your factoring reports just as closely.

## Records to keep

- Rate confirmations, bills of lading and proof of delivery
- Settlement statements and factoring reports
- Fuel receipts and IFTA mileage records
- Repair and maintenance invoices (also useful for DOT and resale)
- Insurance policies and certificates
- Loan, lease and equipment purchase documents
- Business formation papers, EIN letter and annual state filings

Store digital copies in organized folders by year and month. Ask your CPA how long to keep tax records and check FMCSA rules for safety-related records.

## When to hire help

Doing your own books is fine at one truck if you stay consistent. Consider a bookkeeper when you fall behind more than two weeks, add a second truck, or start paying drivers.
