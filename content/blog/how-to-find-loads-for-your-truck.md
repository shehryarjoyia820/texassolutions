Knowing **how to find loads for your truck** is the skill that decides whether an owner-operator business works. A truck that sits costs money every day. A truck that runs cheap freight can lose money while it moves. This guide covers where loads come from, how to find high paying loads, and what to do when there are no loads for your truck.

## Where owner-operators get loads

There are four main sources of freight. Most carriers use more than one.

- **Load boards.** Online marketplaces where brokers post loads. They are fast and open to everyone, so they are also the most competitive. See our guide to [load boards for owner-operators](/blog/best-load-boards-for-owner-operators.html).
- **Broker relationships.** Brokers who know you, your truck and your service will call you before they post a load. This is where consistent loads for owner-operators usually come from.
- **Direct shippers.** Freight straight from the company that ships it, with no broker in the middle. It takes time to win, but it can turn into dedicated lanes.
- **Dispatch services.** A dispatcher searches boards, calls brokers and negotiates rates for you. It is a load booking service that works under your own authority.

## How to get loads for owner-operators, step by step

1. **Know your cost per mile.** You cannot judge a rate until you know your costs. Use the [cost per mile calculator](/cost-per-mile-calculator.html).
2. **Pick your lanes.** Decide where you want to run and where you need to be home. Lanes with freight in both directions reduce empty miles.
3. **Get set up with brokers early.** Carrier packets take time. Being set up before you need a load saves hours.
4. **Search before you deliver.** Look for your next load while you are still loaded. The best time to book out of a market is before you arrive.
5. **Call, do not just click.** Many rates are negotiable. A phone call can move the price and get you details the post does not show.
6. **Keep notes.** Track which brokers paid fast, which loads had long waits, and which lanes paid well.

## How to find high paying loads

A high rate per loaded mile is not always a high paying load. What matters is what the load pays for every mile you drive, including deadhead.

### Worked example: comparing two loads

Your truck is empty in one city. Two loads are offered.

- **Load A:** pays $2,000 for 500 loaded miles. That is $4.00 per loaded mile. But the pickup is 100 miles away.
- **Load B:** pays $1,800 for 480 loaded miles. That is $3.75 per loaded mile. The pickup is 20 miles away.

Now count total miles:

- Load A: 500 + 100 = 600 miles. $2,000 / 600 = **$3.33 per total mile.**
- Load B: 480 + 20 = 500 miles. $1,800 / 500 = **$3.60 per total mile.**

Load B pays $200 less, but it earns 27 cents more per mile you actually drive. It also uses 100 fewer miles of fuel and driving time. Run your own numbers with the [deadhead miles calculator](/deadhead-miles-calculator.html) and the [load profitability calculator](/load-profitability-calculator.html).

Also check where the load drops you. A great rate into a market with little outbound freight can cost you the next day.

## Finding loads by equipment type

Each trailer type has its own freight patterns.

- **Best paying loads for dry van:** dry van is the largest freight pool, so competition is high. Good loads often come from repeat broker lanes rather than the first board post. See [dry van dispatch](/dry-van-dispatch.html).
- **Find reefer loads:** reefer freight often has tight appointments and temperature rules. Ask about pre-cool, pulp temperatures and lumper fees before you book.
- **Find flatbed loads:** flatbed and step deck loads need the right securement gear and tarps. Confirm dimensions, weight and tarp requirements first.
- **Hotshot loads near me:** hotshot freight is often time-sensitive and partial. Check weight limits against your setup and your CDL status.
- **Loads for box trucks:** box truck freight is smaller and more frequent. Liftgate, pallet jack and payload matter. See [box truck dispatch](/box-truck-dispatch.html).

## How to find loads without a load board

Load boards are useful, but you do not have to depend on them.

- **Call brokers you have hauled for.** Ask what lanes they cover every week.
- **Ask for repeat freight.** After a clean delivery, ask the broker if they have the same load next week.
- **Contact local shippers.** Warehouses, manufacturers and distributors near your home base may need a reliable truck.
- **Use a dispatcher with broker contacts.** A dispatcher spends all day on broker networks, not just on one board.

## How to get direct shipper loads and dedicated lanes

Direct shipper loads and dedicated lanes for owner-operators are earned, not found. Shippers care about on-time service, clear communication and a clean safety record.

- Build a track record with brokers first.
- Keep your insurance and authority documents current and ready to send.
- Focus on one or two lanes so a shipper sees you as reliable in their area.
- Be honest about capacity. One truck cannot cover five loads a week across three states.

## No loads for my truck: what to do

Slow days happen. When you are stuck, try this:

1. **Widen the search radius** and look at nearby markets.
2. **Take a short paid load** toward a stronger market instead of deadheading.
3. **Compare waiting with moving.** A day of waiting may cost less than 200 empty miles, or it may not. Do the math.
4. **Call brokers directly** and ask what they have not posted yet.
5. **Get help finding freight for your truck** from a dispatcher who knows the lanes.
