If you have never used a dispatch service, it can be hard to picture what actually happens between "I signed up" and "I got paid." This guide walks through **how truck dispatching works** from the carrier's side of the desk, one step at a time, so you know what to expect and what to ask for.

## What a truck dispatcher is (and is not)

A dispatcher for truckers is an agent who works on behalf of a carrier. The dispatcher finds freight, negotiates rates and handles a lot of the admin that comes with every load. The truck still runs under **your** MC authority, and you are still the carrier of record.

A dispatcher is not a freight broker. A broker holds its own broker authority and represents the shipper's freight. If you want the full comparison, see [truck dispatcher vs freight broker](/blog/truck-dispatcher-vs-freight-broker.html).

## Step 1: Onboarding

Everything starts with paperwork. A good dispatch service collects it once, keeps it organized and sends it out to brokers when needed.

Typical onboarding items:

- MC and USDOT numbers
- Certificate of insurance (auto liability and cargo)
- W-9 for your business
- Signed dispatch agreement that spells out the fee and what it covers
- Factoring company details or bank details for payment
- A notice of assignment, if you factor

You will also talk through how you want to run. This conversation matters more than the paperwork:

- Equipment type, trailer length and any special gear (straps, tarps, load locks, pallet jack)
- Home base and how often you want to be home
- Lanes you like and states you want to avoid
- Your minimum rate per mile, based on your real costs
- Hours of service you have left and your weekly mile target

If your authority is brand new, say so. Some brokers will not work with a new MC, so the dispatcher needs to focus on brokers who do. Texas Solutions works with new authority from day one. See [dispatch for new authority](/dispatch-for-new-authority.html).

## Step 2: Broker setup

Before a broker can tender a load to you, your company has to be set up in that broker's system. That usually means a carrier packet, insurance verification and sometimes an online onboarding portal.

A dispatcher handles the back-and-forth so you are not filling out forms at a truck stop. The more brokers you are set up with, the more freight your dispatcher can offer you. More detail is in our guide to [broker setup and rate confirmations](/blog/broker-setup-rate-confirmation-spot-vs-contract.html).

## Step 3: Load search

This is the part most people picture. The dispatcher searches load boards, calls brokers they already work with and watches for freight that fits your truck.

A good search looks at more than the posted rate:

- **Deadhead to pickup.** Empty miles to the shipper cost fuel and time.
- **Pickup and delivery windows.** Can you legally make it on your hours?
- **Destination market.** Will you be able to get a decent load out of there?
- **Broker payment history.** Fast, reliable pay matters as much as the rate.
- **Weight and commodity.** Does it fit your equipment and insurance?

## Step 4: Negotiation

Posted rates are often a starting point. The dispatcher calls, asks for details the post leaves out, and pushes for a rate that works for your costs. Our [guide to negotiating freight rates](/blog/how-to-negotiate-freight-rates.html) explains the approach.

## Step 5: You approve the load

Before anything is booked, the dispatcher sends you the details: pickup, delivery, miles, weight, rate and any special requirements. **You decide.** At Texas Solutions, the carrier approves every load. No forced dispatch.

## Step 6: Booking and the rate confirmation

Once you say yes, the dispatcher confirms with the broker. The broker sends a **rate confirmation**, the document that lists the agreed rate, addresses, times and terms. Read it before you roll. If something differs from what was discussed, the dispatcher should sort it out before pickup.

## Step 7: On the road

While you drive, the dispatcher can help with:

- Check calls and tracking updates to the broker
- Reporting delays early, so the broker is not surprised
- Documenting detention time at the shipper or receiver
- Arranging lumper receipts when unloading fees come up
- Lining up the next load before you deliver

That last point is where a dispatcher earns a lot of the fee. Searching for your next load while you are still loaded cuts down the hours you sit empty.

## Step 8: Paperwork, invoicing and payment

After delivery, you send the signed bill of lading (BOL) or proof of delivery. Then the money side starts:

- The invoice and paperwork go to the broker or to your factoring company
- Detention and lumper charges are submitted with receipts
- The broker pays on its terms, or the factoring company advances the payment

## Step 9: The dispatch fee

Most dispatchers bill weekly, based on the truck's gross. Here is how that looks with labelled assumptions.

**Worked example: one semi, one week**

- Assumption: 3 loads booked in the week
- Load 1: $3,200
- Load 2: $2,600
- Load 3: $3,100
- Weekly gross: $3,200 + $2,600 + $3,100 = **$8,900**
- Dispatch fee at 5%: $8,900 x 0.05 = **$445**
- Gross after dispatch fee: $8,900 - $445 = **$8,455**

You still pay fuel, insurance, truck payment, maintenance and other costs from that amount. To test a load before you accept it, use the [load profitability calculator](/load-profitability-calculator.html). These numbers are only an illustration. Weekly gross depends on lane, season, equipment and how much you run, and no dispatcher can guarantee it.

## What a week looks like with a dispatcher

Put together, a typical week runs in a loop:

1. Dispatcher searches and negotiates while you are on the road
2. You approve or decline each load
3. Dispatcher books it and sends the rate confirmation
4. You pick up and deliver while the next load is being lined up
5. Paperwork goes out, the broker or factor pays, the fee is billed

## Questions to ask any dispatch service

- Is the fee a percentage of gross, and what exactly is it charged on?
- Are there setup fees, cancellation fees or a long contract?
- Do I approve every load?
- Who handles broker packets, invoicing and detention claims?
- Do you work with new authority?
