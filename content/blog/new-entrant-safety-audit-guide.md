The **new entrant safety audit** is FMCSA's first close look at how a new carrier runs safety. It is not a test of your driving. It is a records review, and most carriers who fail do so because a program was never set up, not because they drove unsafely. If you just got your authority, start preparing now.

## What the new entrant program is

When you get a USDOT number and begin interstate operations, you enter an 18-month monitoring period called the New Entrant Safety Assurance Program. During that window FMCSA watches your roadside inspections and crash record, and conducts a safety audit. If you pass the audit and stay out of trouble, you move to permanent status at the end of the period.

FMCSA generally aims to audit property carriers within the first 12 months. Some carriers hear nothing for months, then get a notice with a short deadline. FMCSA has also discussed changes to the new entrant process over the years, so check the current requirements on the FMCSA website.

If you are still setting up, our guide to [getting your MC number](/blog/how-to-get-an-mc-number.html) covers the steps before this one.

## The areas the auditor reviews

The audit usually covers six areas:

1. **Driver qualification:** a complete DQ file for every driver, including you.
2. **Drug and alcohol testing:** for CDL drivers, a working testing program, random pool enrollment, and Clearinghouse use.
3. **Hours of service:** records of duty status, ELD data and supporting documents.
4. **Vehicle maintenance:** inspection, repair and maintenance records, annual inspections and driver vehicle inspection reports.
5. **Insurance:** proof of the required minimum financial responsibility.
6. **Accident register:** a record of DOT-recordable crashes, even if you have had none.

Hazmat carriers get additional review of hazmat registration, training and shipping papers.

## Violations that cause an automatic fail

FMCSA lists certain violations that, if found, fail the audit by themselves. The list appears in the safety audit evaluation criteria in 49 CFR Part 385 (confirm the current version with FMCSA), but in plain terms they cover:

- **No drug and alcohol testing program**, no random testing, or no pre-employment test before a driver performed safety-sensitive work.
- **Using a driver who tested positive or refused a test** without completing the return-to-duty process.
- **Using a driver without a valid CDL**, or with a suspended, revoked or disqualified license.
- **Using a driver without a current medical certificate.**
- **Operating without the required insurance.**
- **Not requiring drivers to keep hours of service records** or having no system for them.
- **Operating a vehicle placed out of service** before it was repaired.
- **No periodic (annual) inspection** of vehicles.
- Certain **hazmat** failures for hazmat carriers.

Notice the theme. Each of these is a missing system rather than a single bad day. One owner-operator who never enrolled in a random testing consortium can fail on that item alone, even with perfect logs.

## Safety audit checklist: documents to have ready

Gather these in one place, labelled by section, before any notice arrives.

**Company**
- [ ] USDOT and MC numbers, current MCS-150 information
- [ ] Proof of insurance and any required filings
- [ ] Accident register covering the past three years (or since you started)
- [ ] Hazmat registration and training records, if applicable

**Each driver**
- [ ] Complete DQ file (use the [driver qualification file checklist](/blog/driver-qualification-file-checklist.html))
- [ ] Current medical certificate and National Registry verification
- [ ] Valid CDL or license copy

**Drug and alcohol program (CDL drivers)**
- [ ] Written testing policy and proof each driver received it
- [ ] Consortium or third-party administrator enrollment for owner-operators
- [ ] Pre-employment negative results
- [ ] Random selection and testing records
- [ ] Clearinghouse registration, pre-employment and annual queries
- [ ] Supervisor reasonable suspicion training records, if you have supervisors

**Hours of service**
- [ ] ELD records for each driver, typically for the past six months
- [ ] Supporting documents such as fuel receipts, bills of lading and toll records
- [ ] ELD user manual, malfunction instruction sheet and blank paper logs in each truck

**Each vehicle**
- [ ] Maintenance file with vehicle identification, inspection and repair records
- [ ] Current annual inspection report
- [ ] Driver vehicle inspection reports and repair certifications for defects

For the testing setup, see the [drug and alcohol testing program](/blog/drug-and-alcohol-testing-program.html) guide. For daily inspection habits that feed your maintenance file, see the [pre-trip inspection checklist](/blog/pre-trip-inspection-checklist.html).
## How the audit goes, step by step

1. **Notice arrives** by mail or email with a date or document request. Read it closely for deadlines and the format they want.
2. **Organize and send documents** in the order requested. Label files clearly. Send what is asked, not your entire filing cabinet.
3. **Interview.** The auditor may ask how you dispatch, how you track hours, how you schedule maintenance and who handles testing. Answer honestly and briefly.
4. **Findings.** You will be told whether you passed and which violations, if any, were found. Even a pass can include violations you should fix.
5. **Follow-up.** If you failed, a written notice explains the corrective action plan deadline.

## If you fail: the corrective action plan

A failed audit is serious but usually fixable. You will get a deadline to submit a corrective action plan (the exact window depends on carrier type, so check your notice). A good plan:

- Names each violation found.
- Explains the cause in one or two sentences.
- Describes what you changed, with proof: an enrollment certificate, a new policy, an updated file, a maintenance schedule.
- Explains how you will prevent it from happening again, and who is responsible.

Submit it early, keep copies, and follow up to confirm it was accepted. Missing the deadline can lead to revocation of your new entrant registration. If the stakes are high, consider getting help from a compliance consultant or transportation attorney.
