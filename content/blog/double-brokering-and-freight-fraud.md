**Double brokering** is one of the most expensive problems in trucking today. A carrier hauls a load in good faith, then finds out the "broker" never had the right to tender it and the money is gone. Freight fraud also includes identity theft, fake brokers and stolen loads. The good news: most scams rely on the same few tricks, and a simple verification routine stops the majority of them.

## What double brokering is

In legitimate freight, a licensed broker arranges a load with a carrier, and that carrier hauls it. Double brokering happens when someone takes a load and passes it to another carrier without authorization. Common versions:

- **Unauthorized re-brokering.** A carrier accepts a load, then hands it to another carrier and keeps the difference. This breaks the broker agreement and, without broker authority, generally breaks federal law.
- **Fraudulent double brokering.** A scammer poses as a carrier or broker using a stolen or fake identity, books a load, re-brokers it to a real carrier, collects payment from the original broker and disappears. The carrier who hauled it often goes unpaid.

Either way, the shipper loses visibility, insurance may not cover the load, and the hauling carrier is left chasing money.

## Other common freight fraud schemes

- **Carrier identity theft.** Scammers use your MC and DOT number, sometimes after changing contact details on public records, to book loads in your name.
- **Fake broker identities.** Lookalike email domains or spoofed phone numbers pretending to be a real brokerage.
- **Fictitious pickups.** A load is booked to steal the freight itself, often high-value goods.
- **Phishing.** Emails or texts with links to fake login pages for load boards, email or government portals.
- **Bank change requests.** Fake requests to change where payments go, sent to brokers or factoring companies.
- **Upfront fee scams.** Someone asks you to pay a fee to "unlock" a load or join a dispatch list.

## Red flags to watch for

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Email from a free account or a domain slightly different from the broker's real one | Common sign of impersonation |
| Phone number not matching FMCSA records | Could be a fake identity |
| Rate far above the market for the lane | Bait to get you to skip checks |
| Pressure to book immediately without paperwork | Cuts out verification |
| BOL shows a different broker than your rate con | The load may be double brokered |
| Pickup or delivery details change suddenly by text | Possible fictitious pickup or theft |
| Request to pay any fee to get the load | Legit brokers pay carriers, not the reverse |
| New authority with no history offering many high-paying loads | Higher risk, check carefully |

## A verification routine for every load

Use this before you sign any rate confirmation:

1. **Look up the broker's MC with FMCSA.** Confirm broker authority is active and a broker bond or trust is on file.
2. **Contact the broker using details from official records,** not only the number or email that contacted you.
3. **Check the email domain** on the rate con against the broker's known domain.
4. **Run a credit check** through your factoring company or credit service.
5. **Match the rate con to the load.** Broker name, MC and load number should be consistent. See [how to read a rate confirmation](/blog/how-to-read-a-rate-confirmation.html).
6. **Check the BOL at pickup.** If it lists a different broker or carrier, call the broker on the BOL before leaving.
7. **Never pay to get a load.**

## Protect your own identity and accounts

Fraudsters want your authority as much as your truck.

- Turn on multi-factor authentication for email, FMCSA portal access, load boards, factoring portals and banking
- Use a business email on your own domain, and tell brokers which addresses you use
- Check your FMCSA registration details regularly for unexpected changes to contact info
- Never click login links in unexpected emails or texts; type the address yourself
- Verify any bank or payment change by calling a known number
- Limit who has access to your accounts, and remove access when people leave
- Keep your authority and filings current; see [how to keep your MC authority active](/blog/keep-your-mc-authority-active.html)

Brokers will ask for a carrier packet with sensitive documents. Send it only to verified brokers. The [carrier packet checklist](/blog/carrier-packet-checklist.html) lists what is normally required.

## What to do if you suspect fraud

- **Before pickup:** do not haul it. Tell the broker listed on official records what you found.
- **At pickup:** if the BOL does not match, call the broker on the BOL before leaving the shipper.
- **After delivery and unpaid:** collect every document and message, contact the original broker and shipper, and file a complaint with FMCSA.
- **If your identity was used:** notify FMCSA, your insurer, your factoring company and the brokers you work with, and change all passwords. Consider talking to an attorney.
- **If freight was stolen:** call local law enforcement and your insurer right away.

Fast reporting matters. The sooner others know, the less damage a scammer can do with your name.

## Dispatch scams are freight fraud too

Some scammers pose as dispatchers, charge upfront fees and then vanish or book double-brokered loads. A legitimate dispatcher should have a clear written agreement and should not need your login credentials for government portals.
