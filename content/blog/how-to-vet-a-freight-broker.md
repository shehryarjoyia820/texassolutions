Every load you haul is a bet that the broker will pay. Learning **how to vet a freight broker** turns that bet into an informed decision. A few minutes of checks before booking can save you from slow payers, unfair contracts and outright fraud.

## Why vetting matters

When you haul for a broker, you usually extend credit: you deliver now and get paid later. If the broker is slow, disputes everything or goes out of business, you carry the loss. Fraudsters also impersonate real brokers, so a familiar name is not proof. Vetting has two goals: confirm the broker is real and legitimate, and confirm they are likely to pay on time.

## Step 1: Check authority with FMCSA

Search the broker's MC or USDOT number in FMCSA's public records and confirm:

- **Broker authority is active.** Make sure it is broker authority, not only carrier authority. A company with only carrier authority should not be brokering your load.
- **Financial security is on file.** Property brokers must keep a surety bond (BMC-84) or trust fund (BMC-85). The required amount is currently $75,000. Confirm the current figure and status with FMCSA.
- **Legal name and address** match the rate con and setup documents.
- **How long the authority has been active,** and whether it has been revoked or reinstated.

Pending revocation, a cancelled bond or a mismatch in name or address is a reason to stop and ask questions.

## Step 2: Check credit and days to pay

Authority tells you a broker is licensed. Credit tells you whether they pay.

- Run a credit check through your factoring company or a credit service before each load
- Look at average days to pay, not only an approval
- Ask your factor whether the broker has payment complaints or fraud warnings
- Talk to other carriers you trust about their experience

If your factoring company will not approve a broker, take that seriously. Our guide on [working with a factoring company](/blog/working-with-a-factoring-company.html) explains how credit checks fit into your routine.

## Step 3: Verify contact details

Impersonation is common, so verify the people, not just the company.

- Compare the phone number and email domain to the broker's official records and known website
- Watch for free email accounts or domains with a small spelling change
- Call the broker's main number from official records to confirm the agent and load are real
- Be careful when a broker contacts you first with an urgent, high-paying load

See [double brokering and freight fraud](/blog/double-brokering-and-freight-fraud.html) for the common scams that rely on fake contact details.

## Step 4: Read the broker-carrier agreement

The setup agreement governs every load you haul for that broker. Read it before signing, and look closely at:

- **Payment terms:** standard days to pay, quick-pay fees, required documents
- **Fines and deductions:** late fees, tracking failures, missing paperwork
- **Claims:** can they hold payment or deduct claims from unrelated loads?
- **Cargo liability limits** and insurance requirements
- **Non-solicitation:** restrictions on working directly with their shippers
- **Indemnity and dispute terms**

You can ask for changes. Have an attorney review agreements you sign often or that feel one-sided. Our guide on [broker setup and rate confirmations](/blog/broker-setup-spot-vs-contract.html) covers the packet process, and [cargo claims](/blog/cargo-claims-guide.html) explains why claim terms matter.

## Step 5: Watch behavior on the first loads

How a broker acts tells you a lot:

- Do they send a complete rate con promptly, matching what you agreed?
- Do they answer the phone after hours when a problem comes up?
- Do they pay detention and TONU when terms are met?
- Do they pay on time without repeated follow-up?

Keep notes. Over a few loads you will know who is worth repeat business.

## Broker vetting scorecard

Use a simple scorecard to make the decision consistent. Hypothetical example scoring a new broker, 1 point per item:

| Check | Result | Point |
|---|---|---|
| Active broker authority | Yes | 1 |
| Bond or trust on file | Yes | 1 |
| Name and address match rate con | Yes | 1 |
| Credit approved by factor | Yes | 1 |
| Days to pay within your limit | Slow | 0 |
| Contacts verified via official records | Yes | 1 |
| Agreement terms acceptable | Yes | 1 |
| Rate in line with market | Yes | 1 |

Score: 7 of 8. A sensible rule might be: 8 means book, 6-7 means book with caution and a smaller exposure, under 6 means pass. Set your own thresholds, but any failure on authority, bond or verification should be an automatic no.

## Red flags that should stop a booking

- Broker authority inactive, pending revocation or missing bond
- Contact details that do not match official records
- Rate far above market with pressure to commit fast
- Refusal to send a written rate con before dispatch
- Request for an upfront fee from you
- BOL at pickup showing a different broker
- Agreement letting them withhold payment on all loads for any dispute

## Balance risk and opportunity

Vetting is not about avoiding every new broker. It is about knowing your exposure. Diversify across several reliable brokers so one slow payer does not stall your cash flow, and use market rate data so you are not tempted by bait offers.
