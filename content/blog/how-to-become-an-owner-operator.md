Plenty of company drivers think about running their own truck. Some make the jump and never look back. Others find out the hard way that owning a truck is a business, not just a better-paying driving job. This guide explains **how to become an owner-operator** step by step, with the decisions that matter most.

## Owner-operator vs company driver

A company driver is paid by the mile, by the load or by the hour, and the company pays for the truck, fuel, insurance and repairs.

An owner-operator owns or leases the truck and pays those costs. In return, the truck's revenue is yours. The upside is control and earning potential. The downside is that a bad month, a blown engine or a slow freight market comes out of your pocket.

Before you go further, read [how much owner-operators make](/blog/how-much-do-owner-operators-make.html) for an honest look at gross vs net.

## Step 1: Get the license and experience

For a semi truck you need a **Class A CDL**. Many people drive for a company for a year or more first. That time teaches you how freight moves, how to manage hours of service and how to deal with shippers and receivers, while someone else pays for the mistakes.

Smaller equipment is different:

- **Box trucks and straight trucks** under 26,001 lbs GVWR can often be driven without a CDL
- **Hotshot** setups may or may not require a CDL, depending on the combined weight rating of the truck and trailer

Check the weight ratings and your state's rules before you buy anything.

## Step 2: Choose your path: lease-on or your own authority

This is the biggest decision you will make.

### Leasing on to a carrier

You own or lease the truck, but you haul under another carrier's authority.

- **Pros:** the carrier provides authority and often insurance, freight and fuel discounts. Easier to start.
- **Cons:** you usually keep a smaller share of the revenue, you may have limited choice of loads, and you depend on one company.

### Running your own MC authority

You become your own motor carrier.

- **Pros:** you choose your loads, lanes and brokers, and the full rate goes to your business.
- **Cons:** you pay for insurance, compliance and filings, and you have to find your own freight or pay someone to find it.

Many people start leased on, learn the business, then get their own authority.

## Step 3: Get your USDOT number and MC authority

If you choose your own authority, you register with FMCSA for a USDOT number and apply for MC operating authority. You also need a BOC-3 process agent filing and proof of insurance on file before the authority goes active.

We cover each filing in detail in [how to get your MC number and trucking authority](/blog/how-to-get-an-mc-number.html), and the full business setup (UCR, IRP plates, IFTA, ELD, drug and alcohol consortium) in [how to start a trucking company](/blog/how-to-start-a-trucking-company.html). Texas Solutions also offers MC authority setup help as part of its [back office services](/trucking-back-office-services.html).

Keep in mind that a new carrier enters FMCSA's new entrant program, which includes a safety audit, usually within the first 12 months. Keep your logs, maintenance records and driver files in order from day one.

## Step 4: Pick the truck and trailer

Your equipment decides which freight you can haul and what it pays. Rough planning ranges from our desk (not market data, available loads or quotes), which vary by lane and season:

| Equipment | Rough rate per mile |
|---|---|
| Flatbed / step deck | $5 - $7 |
| Reefer | $4 - $6 |
| Hotshot | $4 - $5 |
| Dry van / power only | $3 - $5 |
| Box truck | $1.80 - $3.20 |

Higher rates usually come with higher costs or more work: tarping and securement for flatbed, fuel for the reefer unit, or more expensive equipment. Compare the trade-offs in [dry van vs reefer vs flatbed](/blog/dry-van-vs-reefer-vs-flatbed.html).

When you look at a used truck, budget for a pre-purchase inspection. A cheap truck that spends weeks in the shop is not cheap.

## Step 5: Build a cash reserve

New owner-operators often underestimate how long it takes for money to come in. Brokers commonly pay on 30-day terms or longer unless you use factoring, and the first insurance down payment can be large.

**Worked example: a startup reserve plan (all figures are assumptions for illustration)**

- Insurance down payment: $4,000
- Filings, registration and plates: $1,500
- First month of truck payment: $2,500
- Fuel for the first two weeks: $3,000
- Repair cushion: $5,000
- Total reserve: $4,000 + $1,500 + $2,500 + $3,000 + $5,000 = **$16,000**

Your own numbers could be higher or lower. The point is to price each item before you start, not after. The [cost per mile calculator](/cost-per-mile-calculator.html) helps you work out what you need to earn per mile to cover it all.

## Step 6: Set up how you get paid

Decide early whether you will wait for brokers to pay or use factoring to get paid within a day or two of delivery. Read [freight factoring explained](/blog/freight-factoring-explained.html) before you sign anything, and check the fee, recourse terms and contract length.

## Step 7: Find your first loads

With a new MC number, the first weeks are the hardest. Some brokers want an authority that has been active for a set period. Others will work with new carriers if your paperwork is clean.

Options for getting loads:

- Search load boards and call brokers yourself
- Build relationships with brokers who accept new authority
- Use a dispatch service that books loads under your MC

Our guide on [how to find loads for your truck](/blog/how-to-find-loads-for-your-truck.html) covers each option.

## A quick checklist

- CDL or the right license for your equipment
- Lease-on or own authority decided
- USDOT, MC, BOC-3 and insurance filings complete
- UCR, plates, IFTA and ELD set up
- Truck inspected and cost per mile calculated
- Cash reserve set aside
- Factoring or banking arranged
- Broker setup started
