Summer is when **reefer loads** get tested. High outside temperatures, produce season volume and long waits at hot docks all push the unit and the product to their limits. Most temperature claims trace back to a few avoidable mistakes, and this guide walks through how to prevent them.

## Why summer is harder on reefer freight

Heat works against you from every direction:

- **Heat soaks into the trailer** walls, floor and doors while it sits in the sun.
- **Product may load warm,** especially fresh produce picked in the field.
- **Door openings** at hot docks let in a lot of warm air quickly.
- **The unit runs harder,** burning more fuel and putting more strain on belts, compressors and alternators.
- **Delays cost more,** because every hour of waiting is an hour of heat load.

Summer is also busy season for produce in many regions, which means more freight and often better rates. See our guide on the [freight market calendar](/blog/freight-market-calendar.html) for when produce seasons tend to peak.

## Before you arrive: pre-cool and pre-trip

1. **Read the instructions.** Confirm set point, mode (continuous or cycle), and whether the reading is in Fahrenheit or Celsius. A wrong unit setting is an expensive mistake.
2. **Pre-cool the empty trailer** to the set point before arrival, unless the shipper says otherwise. Give it enough time; on a hot day this can take a while.
3. **Check the unit:** fuel level, belts, coolant, alarms and any active fault codes.
4. **Check the trailer:** door seals, air chute, floor drains, and that the trailer is clean and odor-free. Food shippers can reject a dirty trailer.
5. **Check tires** while cool. Heat raises tire pressure, so cold checks give the honest reading.

## At the shipper: pulp temps and paperwork

The temperature that matters is the product, not the air. Use a calibrated probe thermometer to check **pulp temperature** on a few pallets from different parts of the load, following the shipper's rules on how and whether you can probe.

If the product is warmer than the required temperature:

- Tell the shipping office before you leave.
- Ask them to cool it further, or to note the actual temperature on the bill of lading.
- Write the temperatures you measured and the time on your copy of the BOL.
- Notify your dispatcher and the broker.

A reefer unit is designed to **hold** temperature, not to pull heat out of a trailer full of warm product. If warm product is not documented at pickup, the receiver's rejection may become your claim. Our [cargo claims guide](/blog/cargo-claims-guide.html) explains how claims are decided.

## Loading for airflow

Cold air has to circulate around the product and back to the unit. Watch for:

- **Gap at the front:** do not let pallets block the return air bulkhead.
- **Gap at the top:** leave space between the top of the load and the air chute so air can travel to the rear.
- **Gap at the rear:** a small space at the doors helps air return along the floor.
- **No plastic sheets or loose cardboard** over pallets unless the shipper requires them.
- **Floor channels clear,** so air can move under the load.

If the load is shipper load and count, you may not control the pattern, but you can note poor airflow conditions on the BOL.

## Continuous vs cycle mode

| Mode | How it works | Common use |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous | Unit runs all the time with steady airflow | Many fresh produce and sensitive loads |
| Cycle (start/stop) | Unit shuts down when set point is reached and restarts as needed | Many frozen loads; saves fuel |

**Run the mode the shipper or broker specifies.** If it is not on the paperwork, ask and get the answer in writing. Do not switch modes to save fuel without permission.

## Fuel and the unit on hot days

Summer heat makes the reefer burn more fuel. A hypothetical example: if your unit burns about 0.8 gallons per hour in continuous mode on a hot day, a 30-hour trip uses about 24 gallons. At a hypothetical $4.50 per gallon, that is about $108 of reefer fuel for one load. Price it into your rate, and use the [truck fuel cost calculator](/truck-fuel-cost-calculator.html) for the tractor side.

Practical tips:

- Fill the reefer tank before every load. Running out of reefer fuel is one of the most common causes of temperature failures.
- In many states, reefer units can run on dyed diesel from a separate tank. It must never go into the truck's fuel tank. Check the rules where you fuel, and keep reefer fuel separate on your IFTA records.
- Park in shade when you can, and keep the unit's condenser clear of dirt and debris so it can shed heat.

## On the road and at the receiver

- **Check temperatures** at every stop, and photograph the unit display with a timestamp if possible.
- **Keep doors closed.** If an officer or the receiver needs to open the trailer, note the time.
- **Move promptly at the receiver.** Long waits in heat add risk. Request detention per your rate confirmation.
- **Download or save the temperature record** after delivery. Many units keep a log; some brokers require the download with your paperwork.

### Food safety rules

The FDA's Sanitary Transportation of Human and Animal Food rule sets duties for shippers, carriers and receivers moving food. For carriers this generally means following the shipper's temperature requirements, providing temperature records when asked, keeping equipment clean, and training staff. Check the current requirements with the FDA.

## Summer reefer checklist

- Set point, unit of measure and mode confirmed in writing
- Trailer pre-cooled, clean, and seals checked
- Reefer tank full; unit with no active alarms
- Pulp temps checked and any warm product documented
- Airflow gaps at front, top and rear
- Temperature checked and recorded at each stop
- Temperature log saved after delivery
