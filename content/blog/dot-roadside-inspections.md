**DOT roadside inspections** are a routine part of trucking. How you prepare decides whether one costs you ten minutes or a tow, a lost load and a mark on your CSA record. This guide covers the inspection levels, what officers look at, and a practical checklist.

## Who does roadside inspections

Most roadside inspections are done by state enforcement officers trained to the **North American Standard** used across the US, Canada and Mexico. The Commercial Vehicle Safety Alliance (CVSA) sets the inspection procedures and the out-of-service criteria. Results are uploaded to FMCSA and feed into your Safety Measurement System (CSA) data.

## The CVSA inspection levels

| Level | Name | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| I | North American Standard Inspection | Full driver and vehicle check, including going under the truck |
| II | Walk-Around Inspection | Driver check plus a vehicle walk-around, without getting under the truck |
| III | Driver/Credential Inspection | Driver license, medical card, hours of service, paperwork |
| IV | Special Inspection | A one-time check of a specific item, often for a study |
| V | Vehicle-Only Inspection | Vehicle check without the driver present, often at a carrier location |
| VI | Enhanced Inspection for Radioactive Shipments | Certain radioactive material loads |
| VII | Jurisdictional Mandated Inspection | State-specific programs that do not fit other levels |
| VIII | Electronic Inspection | Data collected electronically without officer contact |

For most owner-operators the common ones are Levels I, II and III.

## What officers check

### The driver

- **Commercial driver's license** valid for the vehicle and any endorsements needed.
- **Medical examiner's certificate** where required. Check current FMCSA and state rules on how medical status is recorded with your license.
- **Hours of service** and the ELD: the officer may request a data transfer and review your record of duty status.
- **ELD documents in the cab:** the user manual, data transfer instructions, malfunction instruction sheet and a supply of blank paper logs.
- **Seat belt use** and signs of fatigue, illness, alcohol or drugs.
- **Shipping papers,** and hazmat documents and placards if applicable.

See our guide to [hours of service rules](/blog/hours-of-service-rules.html) to understand the limits you will be checked against.

### The vehicle

On a Level I, expect the officer to look at:

- **Brakes:** adjustment, air leaks, linings, drums, hoses, and the warning device.
- **Tires and wheels:** tread depth, cuts, bulges, inflation, loose or missing lug nuts.
- **Lights and reflectors:** headlamps, brake lamps, turn signals, clearance lamps.
- **Coupling devices:** fifth wheel, kingpin, safety chains for converter gear or trailers that use them.
- **Steering and suspension:** play, leaks, broken springs.
- **Frame, exhaust and fuel system:** cracks, leaks, loose parts.
- **Cargo securement:** tiedowns, number and condition, blocking and bracing.
- **Windshield, wipers, mirrors** and emergency equipment.
- **Periodic (annual) inspection** proof for the truck and trailer.

Brakes, tires and lights are among the most commonly cited problems, which is also why they are easy wins in a good pre-trip routine.

## Documents to keep in the cab

Use this as a quick check before every trip:

- [ ] CDL and medical certificate (if you carry a paper copy)
- [ ] Truck and trailer registration (cab card)
- [ ] Proof of insurance
- [ ] Annual inspection reports or decals for truck and trailer
- [ ] Lease agreement if running under another carrier's authority
- [ ] ELD manual, data transfer instructions, malfunction sheet, blank paper logs
- [ ] Bill of lading and shipping papers
- [ ] IFTA license and decals, and IRP cab card where applicable
- [ ] Copy of stamped Form 2290 Schedule 1 (often requested at registration and sometimes useful on the road)
- [ ] Permits for oversize, overweight or specific states if needed

## How to handle the inspection itself

1. **Pull in safely and follow instructions.** Have paperwork organized in one folder.
2. **Stay polite and brief.** Answer questions directly. Arguing on the roadside rarely helps.
3. **Do what is asked** during the brake check or lights check.
4. **Read the report** before you leave. Make sure your USDOT number, the VIN, plates and your name are right.
5. **Take photos** of anything you believe is wrong, with time and location.
6. **Fix defects** listed, and keep the repair receipt.
7. **Return the report** to the carrier if you are a leased driver. Many reports need a signed certification that defects were repaired, returned within the time stated on the report.

If something on the report is inaccurate, you can request a review later. See [how to dispute a violation with DataQs](/blog/how-to-dispute-a-violation-dataqs.html).

### Out-of-service orders

Some violations are serious enough that the driver or the truck cannot move until the problem is corrected. Examples include certain brake defects, badly worn or damaged tires, and driving over hours. These are listed in the CVSA North American Standard Out-of-Service Criteria. Our guide to [out-of-service criteria](/blog/out-of-service-criteria.html) explains how it works and what to do.

## Inspection blitzes

CVSA runs enforcement events across North America, such as the yearly International Roadcheck and Brake Safety Week. During these events more trucks are inspected, often at Level I, with a focus area announced in advance. Watch CVSA's announcements and plan extra time.

## A pre-trip routine that prevents violations

A solid 15-minute walk-around catches most problems before an officer does:

1. Lights on, walk around: every lamp, both directions on turn signals, brake lamps.
2. Tires: tread, sidewalls, pressure, lug nuts.
3. Air system: build-up, leaks, low-air warning, brake stroke where you can check it.
4. Coupling: fifth wheel locked, kingpin engaged, glad hands and lines secure.
5. Cargo: tiedowns tight, load not shifted.
6. Paperwork folder in place.

Inspection results feed directly into your [CSA scores](/blog/csa-scores-explained.html), so this routine pays for itself.

This is general information. Inspection procedures and out-of-service criteria are updated, so check CVSA and FMCSA for current rules.
