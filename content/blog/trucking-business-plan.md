A **trucking business plan** is a short, honest document that answers one question: can this truck (or fleet) make money, and how? You do not need fancy language. You need realistic costs, a clear plan for finding freight and a break-even number you can hit. This guide gives you the sections to include, a simple template and a hypothetical example. Have a CPA review your financial projections, especially if you will show the plan to a lender.

## Why write one at all

- **It tests your numbers before you spend money.** Many new carriers discover on paper that their plan only works at rates they cannot get.
- **Lenders may ask for it** when you apply for a truck loan or line of credit.
- **It keeps you focused** when the first slow month hits.
- **It becomes your yardstick.** Each month you compare reality against the plan.

## The sections to include

### 1. Business summary
One or two paragraphs: who you are, your experience, your legal structure, your authority status and what you will haul. Include your CDL years and any specialty experience.

### 2. Services and equipment
Truck and trailer type, year and condition, and the freight it suits. Explain why you chose it. If you are still deciding, compare rates, freight availability and equipment cost for each trailer type in your area.

### 3. Market and lanes
Where you will run, what kinds of shippers and brokers move that freight, and how seasonal it is. Be specific: home base, typical lanes, how often you plan to be home.

### 4. How you will find loads
Load boards, direct shippers, broker relationships, a dispatcher, or a mix. A new authority often faces broker restrictions at first, so plan for that. Our guide on [how to get loads with a new MC number](/blog/how-to-get-loads-with-new-mc-number.html) covers the early months.

### 5. Operations and compliance
Insurance, ELD, drug and alcohol testing program, maintenance plan, IFTA and IRP, and who handles paperwork. Lenders and partners want to see you understand the rules.

### 6. Startup costs
Everything you must pay before the first load is paid for.

### 7. Monthly operating budget
Fixed costs (truck payment, insurance, permits, ELD) and variable costs (fuel, maintenance, tolls, dispatch and factoring fees).

### 8. Revenue projection and break-even
Expected miles, realistic rate per total mile, and the minimum you must earn to cover costs.

### 9. Cash reserve and risk plan
How many months of fixed costs you can cover with no revenue, and what you will do if the truck is down.

### 10. Growth plan
What has to be true before you add a second truck. See [how to grow from 1 to 5 trucks](/blog/grow-from-1-to-5-trucks.html).

## Worked example: hypothetical startup and break-even

All figures are **hypothetical** to show the method, not real costs.

**Startup costs**

| Item | Hypothetical amount |
|---|---|
| Truck down payment | $15,000 |
| Authority, registrations and filings | $1,500 |
| First insurance down payment | $4,000 |
| ELD, phone and setup | $800 |
| Initial repairs and supplies | $2,500 |
| Cash reserve (fixed costs plus a repair and living buffer) | $18,000 |
| **Total** | **$41,800** |

**Monthly fixed costs (hypothetical):** truck payment $2,000, insurance $1,600, ELD and software $150, permits and other $250. Total: **$4,000 a month**. In this example the $18,000 reserve covers three months of fixed costs ($12,000) plus a $6,000 buffer for repairs and slow weeks. Adjust it to your situation.

**Variable costs (hypothetical):** fuel $0.85 per mile, maintenance $0.15 per mile, other variable $0.20 per mile. Total: **$1.20 per mile**.

**Break-even at 9,000 miles a month:**

- Fixed cost per mile: $4,000 / 9,000 = about $0.44
- Total cost per mile: $0.44 + $1.20 = about **$1.64**
- Revenue needed just to break even: $1.64 x 9,000 = about **$14,800**

Then add the owner's pay and a profit target. If you need $6,000 a month to live and want $1,500 profit, you need about $22,300 a month, or roughly $2.48 per total mile at 9,000 miles. Is that realistic for your freight and lanes? That is the real question the plan answers. Run your own version in the [break-even calculator](/break-even-calculator.html) and [cost per mile calculator](/cost-per-mile-calculator.html).

## A one-page template you can copy

```
BUSINESS SUMMARY: name, structure, owner experience, authority status
EQUIPMENT: truck, trailer, freight type
LANES: home base, main lanes, home time plan
LOAD SOURCES: boards / brokers / direct / dispatcher
COMPLIANCE: insurance, ELD, testing program, IFTA/IRP, maintenance
STARTUP COSTS: itemized total
MONTHLY BUDGET: fixed $____ + variable $____/mile
BREAK-EVEN: $____/mile at ____ miles/month
TARGET: owner pay $____ + profit $____ = $____/month
RESERVE: ____ months of fixed costs
GROWTH TRIGGER: what must be true before truck #2
```

## Common business plan mistakes

- Using your best-ever rate as the average
- Forgetting deadhead miles in the revenue math
- Leaving out the owner's own pay
- Skipping the cash reserve
- Assuming the truck will never break down
- Never updating the plan once real numbers arrive
