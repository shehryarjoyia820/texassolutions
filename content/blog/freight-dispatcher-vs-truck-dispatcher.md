Search for help finding loads and you will see a pile of job titles: **freight dispatcher**, truck dispatcher, trucking dispatcher, cargo dispatcher, load planner. If you run your own authority, it is fair to wonder whether these are different services with different prices.

For a carrier in the US trucking industry, they are almost always the same thing. This post sorts out the names, explains what the work looks like day to day, and shows where the real lines are drawn: between a dispatcher and a broker, and between an outside service and in-house fleet dispatch.

## Why there are so many names for one job

The labels grew out of habit, region and marketing more than any legal definition. FMCSA does not license a "freight dispatcher" or a "truck dispatcher" as separate categories.

Here is how the terms usually get used:

- **Truck dispatcher / trucking dispatcher.** Named after the equipment. The focus is on keeping a specific truck loaded and moving.
- **Freight dispatcher.** Named after what the truck hauls. Same job, described from the load side.
- **Cargo dispatcher.** A less common variation, sometimes used by people coming from shipping or logistics backgrounds.
- **Freight dispatch, cargo dispatch, trucking dispatch, dispatch trucking.** Names for the service itself rather than the person.

So when a company advertises freight dispatching and another advertises truck dispatching, you are comparing the same kind of service. Judge them on who they work for, what they include and how they charge.

## What a freight dispatcher does for a carrier

We cover the full load cycle in [how truck dispatching works](/blog/how-does-truck-dispatching-work.html), so here is a shorter view of the daily routine.

### Morning: planning the next move

Freight dispatchers rarely just sit on a load board waiting for something to appear. A good one starts the day knowing where each truck will empty and how many hours each driver has left. From there they decide which markets to search, and whether it makes sense to reposition before booking.

### Midday: finding and pricing freight

Most of the day goes into dispatching freight: searching load boards, calling brokers they already have relationships with, and asking questions the posting leaves out. The goal is a load that fits the equipment, the hours and the carrier's minimum rate, with a sensible reload market at the other end.

### Booking with your approval

Once a load looks right, the dispatcher sends you the details. Nothing gets booked until you agree. After you say yes, they confirm with the broker and check the rate confirmation.

### While the truck is loaded

Dispatching trucks does not stop at the booking. Day to day, a dispatcher also:

- Handles broker setup packets so you can haul for new brokers quickly
- Sends updates when a pickup or delivery runs late
- Tracks detention time and collects lumper receipts
- Sends paperwork for invoicing or to your factoring company
- Looks for the next load before you deliver, to keep empty miles down

## Freight dispatcher vs freight broker

This is the line that actually matters. A dispatcher works for the carrier. A broker works for the shipper's freight and earns the spread between the shipper's rate and what you get paid.

Because a dispatcher is usually paid a percentage of your gross, the dispatcher earns more only when you earn more. A broker's margin moves the other way. That difference is covered in detail in [truck dispatcher vs freight broker](/blog/truck-dispatcher-vs-freight-broker.html), so we will not repeat it here. Put simply, a dispatcher helps you find and dispatch freight for your own truck, while a broker places a shipper's freight with whichever carrier will take it.

One warning sign: if a "dispatch" company wants to take payment from shippers and pay you a lower amount, it is acting like a broker, whatever it calls itself.

## Outside dispatch vs in-house fleet dispatch

Large fleets often have their own dispatch department. Those dispatchers are employees who plan only that company's trucks, often around contract freight and company-driver schedules.

An outside dispatch service is built for carriers who do not want to hire that staff:

| | In-house fleet dispatch | Outside dispatch service |
|---|---|---|
| Who they work for | One fleet, as employees | Many independent carriers |
| How they are paid | Salary or wages from the fleet | Usually a percentage of each truck's gross |
| Who chooses loads | The fleet's planners | The carrier approves each load |
| Best fit | Large fleets with steady freight | Owner-operators and small fleets |

For a single truck or a small fleet, paying a full-time dispatcher often does not add up. A percentage-based truck dispatch service scales with the truck instead. If you run several trucks, see our [small fleet dispatch](/small-fleet-dispatch.html) page.

## How to pick a freight dispatch service

Since the title tells you little, look at how the service operates. Ask these questions before you sign anything:

1. **Do I approve every load?** Forced dispatch is a red flag.
2. **What is the fee charged on?** Get the percentage and what counts as gross in writing.
3. **Are there setup fees or a long contract?** You should be able to leave if it is not working.
4. **Do you work with a new MC?** Many brokers avoid new authorities, so this takes extra effort.
5. **What back office work is included?** Broker packets, invoicing, detention and lumper collection make a real difference to your week.
6. **Are you a broker too?** You want someone clearly on the carrier side.
7. **Who answers at night or on weekends?** Trucks break down and appointments change outside office hours.

Also check the math on your own numbers. Know your cost per mile before you talk to anyone, using the [cost per mile calculator](/cost-per-mile-calculator.html), so you can tell whether the loads being offered actually pay. ## Matching the service to your equipment

The work is the same across equipment types, but the freight is not. A box truck dispatcher spends more time on local and regional freight and tighter appointment windows. A hotshot dispatcher deals with partial loads and weight limits. A semi dispatcher juggles dry van, reefer, flatbed or power only markets.
