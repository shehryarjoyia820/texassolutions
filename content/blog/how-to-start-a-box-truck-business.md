A box truck business is one of the lower-cost ways to own a trucking company. The truck costs less than a tractor and trailer, many box trucks do not require a CDL, and there is steady demand for freight that is too small or too awkward for a 53 ft trailer. The trade-off is that margins are thinner, so the planning you do before the first load matters.

This guide walks through the steps in order. If you are still deciding between a box truck and a semi, our general guide on [how to start a trucking company](/blog/how-to-start-a-trucking-company.html) covers the bigger picture.

## Step 1: Decide what kind of freight you want to haul

Before you shop for a truck, decide how you plan to earn money with it. Box truck freight usually falls into a few groups:

- **OTR and regional spot freight** booked through load boards and brokers.
- **Expedited and partial loads** that need to move faster than a full truckload.
- **Local delivery and final-mile work** for retailers, furniture and appliance sellers, and distributors.
- **Repeat lanes and contracts** that grow out of good service with the same brokers or shippers.

The type of freight you chase decides the truck you need, the insurance you buy and whether a dispatcher makes sense for you.

## Step 2: Choose the right box truck

Most box truck owner-operators settle on a **26 ft box truck with a liftgate**. That size typically holds around 12 standard pallets depending on how they are loaded, works for most brokers' "straight truck" postings, and many models stay at a GVWR of 26,000 lbs or less.

When you compare trucks, look at:

- **GVWR.** At 26,000 lbs or less, a single truck generally does not require a CDL. At 26,001 lbs or more, it generally needs a Class B CDL. Our guide to [non-CDL box truck and hotshot loads](/blog/non-cdl-box-truck-and-hotshot-loads.html) explains the thresholds in detail.
- **Payload.** GVWR minus the truck's empty weight is what you can legally haul. A heavy body and liftgate reduce it.
- **Liftgate.** Many shippers and receivers without docks require one. Without it you will pass on a lot of freight.
- **Door type and interior.** Roll-up versus swing doors, E-track and logistics posts for strapping, and interior height.
- **Fuel and maintenance history.** A cheap truck that spends weeks in the shop is not cheap.

## Step 3: Set up the business

Treat it as a real company from day one:

- Form a business entity (many owner-operators use an LLC) and get an EIN from the IRS.
- Open a separate business bank account.
- Pick a simple bookkeeping method so you can track fuel, repairs and income per load.

Talk with an accountant about the entity and tax setup that fits your situation.

## Step 4: Get your USDOT number and operating authority

For interstate for-hire work, you will generally need:

- **A USDOT number** from FMCSA.
- **Interstate operating authority** (commonly called an MC number) for hauling regulated freight for hire.
- **A BOC-3 filing** naming process agents in each state.
- **Unified Carrier Registration (UCR)**, renewed each year.

FMCSA updates its registration system from time to time, so follow the current instructions on its website. Our post on [how to get an MC number](/blog/how-to-get-mc-number-authority-owner-operator.html) covers the process, and Texas Solutions offers [MC authority setup](/trucking-back-office-services.html) as part of its back office services if you would rather not do the filings yourself.

Drivers of commercial vehicles over 10,000 lbs in interstate commerce generally need a DOT medical card even without a CDL, and hours-of-service rules apply. Check what applies to your operation.

## Step 5: Buy the right insurance

Your authority will not activate until the required liability coverage is on file with FMCSA. Beyond the federal minimum, brokers set their own requirements, and many ask for higher auto liability limits plus cargo coverage. Get quotes from agents who write commercial trucking policies, and ask each one what limits brokers in your target freight usually require.

Insurance is often a new carrier's largest fixed cost, so get quotes before you buy the truck.

## Step 6: Get loads with new authority

This is where many new box truck businesses stall. Some brokers will not work with an MC that has only been active for a short time. You can still get loaded, but it takes more calls and more paperwork.

Ways to find your first loads:

- **Load boards.** Filter for straight trucks or box trucks. See our guide to the [best load boards for box trucks](/blog/best-load-boards-for-box-trucks.html).
- **Brokers who accept new authority.** Build a list and keep your setup packet ready to send.
- **A box truck dispatcher with new authority experience.** A dispatcher already knows which brokers take new carriers and can fill out the setup packets for you.

## A simple startup cost check

Every business is different, so build your own numbers. Here is a sample way to lay them out, using assumed figures only:

- Truck down payment (assumption): $10,000
- First insurance down payment (assumption): $3,000
- Authority, BOC-3, UCR and registrations (assumption): $1,000
- ELD, phone, basic equipment like straps and blankets (assumption): $1,000
- Cash reserve for fuel and repairs while waiting on first payments (assumption): $5,000

Total in this example: $10,000 + $3,000 + $1,000 + $1,000 + $5,000 = **$20,000**.

The reserve matters more than most people expect. Brokers often pay on 30-day terms or longer unless you use [factoring](/blog/freight-factoring-for-owner-operators-explained.html), so you need cash to cover fuel before the first payments arrive.

## Step 7: Know your cost per mile before you book

Box truck rates often run about **$1.80 to $3.20 per mile**, depending on lane and season. That range is wide, and the low end may not cover your costs. Work out your own number with the [cost per mile calculator](/cost-per-mile-calculator.html), then check each offer with the [load profitability calculator](/load-profitability-calculator.html).

If your all-in cost is $1.50 a mile and a load pays $1.90 a loaded mile with 80 empty miles to the pickup on a 300-mile trip, the math looks like this:

- Revenue: 300 x $1.90 = $570
- Cost: (300 + 80) x $1.50 = $570
- Profit: **$0**, before dispatch or factoring fees

That load only looks good if you ignore the empty miles.
