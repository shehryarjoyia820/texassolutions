Brokers move a large share of the freight small carriers haul. That is not a bad thing, but many owner-operators wonder whether they could keep more of the rate by cutting out the middle. This guide explains **how to get loads without a broker**, what it really takes, and where it makes sense.

## Why carriers look for direct freight

The reasons are usually the same:

- **No broker margin.** The shipper's full rate goes to the carrier.
- **Steadier freight.** A shipper that likes your service may give you the same lanes every week.
- **Better planning.** Knowing your loads in advance makes home time and maintenance easier to schedule.

Those benefits are real, but direct freight is not free money. It comes with its own work and risk.

## Option 1: Direct shippers

A direct shipper is the company that owns the freight: a manufacturer, distributor, farm, food plant or building supply yard.

### How to approach shippers

1. **Start local and in your lanes.** Look for businesses near your home base or along routes you already run.
2. **Find the right person.** Ask for the shipping manager, logistics coordinator or traffic department.
3. **Bring your paperwork.** Authority, insurance certificate, W-9 and a short summary of your equipment and service area.
4. **Offer a specific lane.** "I can run your freight from here to that city twice a week" is easier to say yes to than "do you have anything?"
5. **Follow up.** Shippers often say no the first time. A carrier who checks in politely and shows up when needed is remembered.

### What shippers expect

- On-time pickups and deliveries, every time
- Clean equipment that fits their freight
- Clear communication about delays
- Proper insurance, sometimes above the levels brokers ask for
- A safety record they can check on FMCSA's public data

New carriers often find this hard at first. Many build a track record with brokers before shippers take them seriously.

## Option 2: Contracts and dedicated lanes

A contract or dedicated lane is a standing agreement to haul freight on a lane at an agreed rate for a period of time.

- **Pros:** predictable volume and rate, easier planning
- **Cons:** the rate is fixed even when the spot market rises, and you may be expected to cover loads even when it is inconvenient

Contracts come from shippers directly, and sometimes from brokers who manage a shipper's freight. For the difference between contract and spot pricing, see [spot vs contract rates](/blog/broker-setup-spot-vs-contract.html).

## Option 3: Amazon Relay and similar platforms

Amazon Relay is Amazon's platform for booking its own freight with carriers who hold their own authority. Loads are booked through the platform rather than through a broker, and some freight uses Amazon trailers, which suits power only operators.

Keep it in perspective:

- Requirements, rates and rules are set by Amazon and can change
- Some loads pay well and others do not, so judge each load on its own numbers
- Depending on a single customer for all your freight is a risk

## The honest pros and cons of direct freight

| | Direct freight | Broker freight |
|---|---|---|
| Rate | Often higher, no middle margin | Includes broker margin |
| Speed to start | Slow, takes relationship building | Fast, available on day one |
| Payment terms | Can be 30 to 60 days or longer | Varies, many offer quick pay |
| Paperwork and billing | You invoice and chase payment | Broker handles shipper side |
| Flexibility | Less, you are committed | More, you pick load by load |
| Backhauls | Shipper may not have any | Easy to find on boards |

## What high paying freight loads really look like

The best paying loads for owner-operators are the ones that pay well **per total mile**, including deadhead, and do not tie your truck up for days. A high rate from a shipper that pays in 60 days may be worse for your cash flow than a slightly lower broker rate paid in two days.

**Worked example: direct vs broker load (assumptions labelled)**

- Direct shipper load: 600 loaded miles at $3.50 a mile = 600 x $3.50 = **$2,100**, paid in 45 days
- Broker load: 600 loaded miles at $3.20 a mile = 600 x $3.20 = **$1,920**, paid quickly through factoring
- Assumed factoring fee on the broker load: 3% of $1,920 = $57.60, leaving **$1,862.40**
- Difference: $2,100 - $1,862.40 = **$237.60** more for the direct load

The direct load pays more, but you wait about six weeks for it. If you need that cash to buy fuel next week, you might factor the direct invoice too, which narrows the gap. Run your own numbers with the [load profitability calculator](/load-profitability-calculator.html).

## How to keep trucks full while you build direct freight

Direct customers rarely fill every mile. Most owner-operators keep a mix:

- **Direct freight** for the main lane
- **Broker freight** for backhauls and gaps
- **Load boards** to check market rates and fill last-minute openings

Our guide to [finding loads for your truck](/blog/how-to-find-loads-for-your-truck.html) covers how to blend these sources. Keeping deadhead low between them matters just as much. The [deadhead miles calculator](/deadhead-miles-calculator.html) shows what empty miles do to your real rate.
