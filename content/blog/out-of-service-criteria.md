An **out-of-service** (OOS) order is the most expensive result a roadside inspection can bring. The driver or truck is stopped until the problem is fixed, the load runs late, and your safety record takes a heavier hit than for an ordinary violation. Knowing the out-of-service criteria helps you catch these problems first.

## What the out-of-service criteria are

The **North American Standard Out-of-Service Criteria** are published by the Commercial Vehicle Safety Alliance (CVSA). They list the conditions that make a driver or vehicle an imminent hazard, so inspectors in the US, Canada and Mexico apply the same standard.

Two things to understand:

- **Not every violation is OOS.** Many violations are recorded and must be fixed, but you can keep driving. OOS conditions are the serious ones.
- **The criteria change.** CVSA reviews and updates them on a regular basis, usually yearly. The examples below are general; for exact measurements and current wording, check the current CVSA criteria or ask your shop.

## Common driver out-of-service conditions

| Area | Examples that can lead to driver OOS |
|---|---|
| Hours of service | Driving past the 11-hour, 14-hour or 60/70-hour limits; no record of duty status when required |
| Records | Falsified logs in certain situations; ELD problems without required backup records |
| License | No valid CDL, wrong class, suspended or revoked license, missing required endorsement |
| Medical | Not medically qualified in certain cases where required |
| Alcohol and drugs | Any detected alcohol or possession or use of drugs while on duty |
| Fitness | Driver too ill or fatigued to drive safely |

Hours-of-service OOS orders typically last until the driver has taken enough off-duty time to be legal. Our guide to [hours of service rules](/blog/hours-of-service-rules.html) helps you plan legal days.

## Common vehicle out-of-service conditions

| System | What can trigger OOS |
|---|---|
| Brakes | A certain percentage of brakes defective or out of adjustment; inoperative brakes on steering axle; major air leaks |
| Tires | Tread worn below the OOS limit (stricter on steer tires); flat or audibly leaking tires; exposed cords or bulges |
| Lights | Required lamps not working when lights are needed, such as at night |
| Coupling | Fifth wheel not locked or with serious defects; missing or damaged safety devices |
| Steering | Excessive steering wheel lash or loose components |
| Suspension | Broken leaf springs beyond limits; cracked or missing parts |
| Frame | Cracked or broken frame members beyond limits |
| Cargo securement | Not enough tiedowns, damaged tiedowns, or loads that can shift |
| Fuel and exhaust | Fuel leaks; exhaust leaks into the cab in certain cases |

Brakes are usually near the top of the list for vehicle OOS violations, so a good brake check on every pre-trip is one of the best habits you can build.

## What happens when you are placed out of service

1. **The officer completes a report** that lists the OOS violations.
2. **The driver or vehicle may not move** until the condition is corrected. For many vehicle defects that means an on-site repair by a mobile mechanic or a tow.
3. **The repair is documented.** Keep invoices and parts receipts.
4. **The report goes into FMCSA's data** and is weighted more heavily in your [CSA scores](/blog/csa-scores-explained.html).
5. **The carrier must certify repairs** and return the report as instructed, usually within a set number of days.

Operating a vehicle or driving in violation of an OOS order is a serious federal violation with large civil penalties and possible disqualification for drivers. Never move a truck under an OOS order unless an officer has cleared it.

## Worked example: the real cost of one OOS

Here is a hypothetical example to show why prevention pays. All figures are for illustration only.

- Load pays: $2,400
- Truck is placed OOS for a brake problem at 9 a.m.
- Mobile repair: $650, truck back on the road at 3 p.m.
- Six hours lost means a missed appointment; the receiver reschedules for next morning and you lose the reload you had planned. Say that costs $500 in lost margin.
- Total hit: about **$1,150**, plus a heavier violation on your record for the next two years.

A pre-trip check that found the problem the night before may have cost a fraction of that at a regular shop.

## How to avoid out-of-service violations

### Daily

- Walk around with lights on. Check every lamp.
- Look at tires: tread, sidewalls, inflation.
- Check brakes: listen for leaks, look at slack adjusters and chambers, test the low-air warning.
- Confirm the fifth wheel is locked and the kingpin is engaged.
- Check tiedowns and load after the first miles and at every stop as required for your freight. Open-deck loads need extra attention to tiedown count and condition.

### Weekly and monthly

- Keep a preventive maintenance schedule and stick to it. Our [maintenance budget calculator](/maintenance-budget-calculator.html) helps you set money aside.
- Replace tires before they reach the limit, not at it.
- Have brakes inspected and adjusted by a qualified technician.

### Paperwork and hours

- Keep your license, medical certificate and endorsements current.
- Make sure the ELD works and you know the malfunction procedure.
- Plan trips so you are not running out of hours far from parking.

## If you believe the OOS was wrong

Take photos, keep the report and get a written opinion from a mechanic if relevant. You can request a review of inaccurate records through FMCSA's DataQs system. Our guide on [how to dispute a violation with DataQs](/blog/how-to-dispute-a-violation-dataqs.html) walks through the process.

This guide is general information. Out-of-service criteria and penalties change, so confirm current rules with CVSA and FMCSA.
