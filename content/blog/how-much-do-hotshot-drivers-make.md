Hotshot trucking has a reputation for strong pay per mile, and it can earn well. But a high rate per mile does not automatically mean high take-home pay. What you keep depends on how many paid miles you run, how many empty miles you burn and what your setup costs to own and insure.

This post focuses on hotshot **owner-operators** who run under their own authority or lease to a carrier. Company drivers are paid differently, usually a wage or a percentage, and do not carry the truck's costs.

## What hotshot loads pay

As a rough guide, hotshot loads often pay about **$4 to $5 a mile**. Factors that push the rate up or down:

- **Urgency.** Same-day or next-morning delivery pays a premium.
- **Weight and size.** Heavier or longer pieces that need a 40 ft deck or more securement often pay more.
- **Lane.** Loads into areas with little return freight need to pay for the trip home.
- **Season and industry.** Construction, agriculture and energy activity swing demand.

For a deeper look see [hotshot trucking rates per mile and fuel](/blog/hotshot-trucking-rates-per-mile-fuel-and-dispatch.html), and compare with other equipment on our [rate per mile guide](/truck-dispatch-rates.html). Rates are never guaranteed.

## Example weekly gross (hypothetical)

As a hypothetical example, OTR hotshots might gross about **$7,000 to $9,000 a week**. That figure is not a guarantee. Weeks with repairs, weather, home time or slow freight come in lower.

## Worked example: a good week

These are assumptions for illustration. Use your own numbers.

**Assumptions:**

- Weekly gross: **$8,000**
- Loaded miles: 1,800, empty miles: 450, total 2,250
- Average fuel economy across loaded and empty miles: 11 mpg
- Diesel price: $4.50 a gallon
- Dispatch: 8% of gross
- Factoring: 3% of gross
- Insurance: $400 a week
- Truck and trailer payments: $600 a week
- Maintenance and tire reserve: $0.20 a mile
- ELD, phone, permits, tolls and other: $120 a week

**Costs:**

- Fuel: 2,250 / 11 = 204.5 gallons x $4.50 = **$920**
- Dispatch: $8,000 x 0.08 = **$640**
- Factoring: $8,000 x 0.03 = **$240**
- Insurance: **$400**
- Payments: **$600**
- Maintenance reserve: 2,250 x $0.20 = **$450**
- Other: **$120**
- **Total: $3,370**

**Net before taxes:** $8,000 - $3,370 = **$4,630**, or about 58% of gross.

## Worked example: a slow week

**Assumptions:** weekly gross **$5,000**, total miles 1,400, everything else unchanged.

- Fuel: 1,400 / 11 = 127.3 gallons x $4.50 = **$573**
- Dispatch: **$400**
- Factoring: **$150**
- Insurance: **$400**
- Payments: **$600**
- Maintenance reserve: 1,400 x $0.20 = **$280**
- Other: **$120**
- **Total: $2,523**

**Net before taxes:** $5,000 - $2,523 = **$2,477**, or about 50% of gross.

Fixed costs stayed at $1,000 a week in both examples. In a slow week they take a bigger bite, which is why a cash reserve is part of running a hotshot business.

## From weekly numbers to a yearly estimate

Avoid multiplying a great week by 52. Instead:

1. Pick a realistic average week, somewhere between your good and slow weeks.
2. Count only the weeks you expect to work.
3. Set aside money for income and self-employment taxes.

For example, if your average net is **$3,500 a week** (an assumption) and you work **46 weeks**, that is 3,500 x 46 = **$161,000 before taxes**. Change either number and the result moves a lot, so run your own scenario.

## Own authority vs leased on

How you run also changes what you keep:

- **Own authority.** You book loads under your own MC, keep the full rate and pay all costs, including insurance and compliance. Income has the most upside and the most risk.
- **Leased to a carrier.** You run under another company's authority and usually receive a percentage of the load. The carrier may cover some costs, such as liability insurance, but takes a share of every load.

Neither is right for everyone. Compare the two with your own numbers before deciding, and if you plan to get your own authority, see [how to get an MC number](/blog/how-to-get-mc-number-authority-owner-operator.html).

## What separates higher-earning hotshot operators

There is no secret, but some habits make a clear difference:

### They know their cost per mile

If you do not know your break-even, you cannot tell a good load from a bad one. Use the [cost per mile calculator](/cost-per-mile-calculator.html) and check each offer with the [load profitability calculator](/load-profitability-calculator.html).

### They plan the reload

A $1,500 load into a dead area that leaves you 250 miles empty may pay less than a $1,200 load into a busy market. See [how to reduce deadhead](/blog/what-is-deadhead-in-trucking-how-to-reduce-it.html).

### They watch weight and fuel

Hotshot fuel economy drops as weight goes up. Track your real mpg loaded and empty, then check your cost by state with the [truck fuel cost calculator](/truck-fuel-cost-calculator.html).

### They get paid for their time

Detention and layovers should be documented and billed. If you do not ask, you usually do not get paid.

### They negotiate

Brokers expect a counteroffer. Our guide on [how to negotiate freight rates](/blog/how-to-negotiate-freight-rates.html) explains how to approach it.
