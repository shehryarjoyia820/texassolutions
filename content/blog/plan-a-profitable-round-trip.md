A single high-paying load can look great on a load board and still lose money once you count the trip home. The fix is to **plan a profitable round trip**: price the full loop, not just the first leg. This guide walks through how owner-operators and small fleets do that in practice.

## Think in loops, not loads

Every load you take puts your truck in a new market. That market decides what your next load pays and how far you drive empty to get it. So the real question is never "Is this load good?" It is "Is this load good, plus whatever it takes to get the truck loaded again?"

Two terms help here:

- **Headhaul:** the strong direction of a lane, with lots of freight and higher rates.
- **Backhaul:** the return direction, with fewer loads and lower rates.

Most profitable loops pair a well-paid headhaul with a backhaul that at least covers its own costs.

## Six steps to plan the loop

### Step 1: Know your cost per mile

You cannot judge a loop without knowing what a mile costs you. Add fixed costs (truck payment, insurance, permits) and variable costs (fuel, tires, maintenance) and divide by miles driven. Use the [cost per mile calculator](/cost-per-mile-calculator.html).

This number is your floor. Any loop that averages below it loses money, no matter how good one leg looks.

### Step 2: Check the delivery market before you book

Before accepting a load, look at the city where it delivers:

- How many loads are posted outbound from that area for your trailer type?
- What do those loads pay compared to the inbound rate?
- How far would you likely deadhead to reach a decent pickup?

If the delivery area has few outbound loads, the inbound load needs to pay enough to cover the weak return. That is a strong point when you [negotiate freight rates](/blog/how-to-negotiate-freight-rates.html).

### Step 3: Price the whole loop

Add every mile and every dollar in the trip:

1. Deadhead to the first pickup.
2. Loaded miles on the first leg.
3. Deadhead to the second pickup.
4. Loaded miles on the return leg.
5. Any deadhead to get home or to the next pickup.

Then divide total revenue by total miles. That is your round-trip rate per mile.

**Worked example (hypothetical)**

| Leg | Miles | Revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Deadhead to pickup A | 50 | $0 |
| Load A (headhaul) | 900 | $2,700 |
| Deadhead to pickup B | 120 | $0 |
| Load B (backhaul) | 850 | $1,700 |
| Total | 1,920 | $4,400 |

Load A pays $3.00 per loaded mile and load B only $2.00. But the round trip earns $4,400 over 1,920 miles, or about $2.29 per total mile. If your cost is $1.85 per mile, the loop clears roughly $0.44 per mile, about $850 before your own pay and taxes.

Now compare: if load B were not available and you deadheaded 600 miles home empty, the loop would be $2,700 over 1,550 miles, about $1.74 per mile. That is below cost. The cheaper backhaul made the trip profitable.

Run your own loops with the [load profitability calculator](/load-profitability-calculator.html).

### Step 4: Use triangle routes when the return lane is weak

When the direct return lane is weak, a triangle route can keep the truck loaded. Instead of going A to B and back to A, you go A to B, B to C, and C back to A, where C is a market with better freight.

Triangle routes work best when:

- The middle market is a strong freight area for your equipment.
- The extra loaded miles pay more than the deadhead you avoid.
- You still have enough hours to run it legally under hours of service rules.

### Step 5: Fit the loop into your hours and home time

A loop is only as good as your ability to run it legally and on time. Check:

- **Hours of service:** daily driving limits and the weekly cycle can turn a two-day loop into three.
- **Appointment times:** tight delivery windows can leave you waiting for a reload.
- **Home time:** a loop that strands you far from home on a Friday may cost you a weekend or an expensive deadhead.

The [hours of service calculator](/hours-of-service-calculator.html) helps you check whether a plan fits before you book.

### Step 6: Line up reloads with brokers you trust

The easiest reload is one a broker offers you before it hits the board. After a smooth delivery, tell the broker where you will be empty and when. Brokers who move freight both ways on a lane may have a return load, and repeat business often comes with better communication and faster payment.

Keep a short list of brokers and shippers by market. Over time, that list becomes your own round-trip map, and you spend less time searching every time the truck empties.

## Common round-trip mistakes

- **Chasing the top rate into a dead market.** The inbound load pays well because few trucks want to go there.
- **Ignoring deadhead.** Empty miles still burn fuel, tires and hours.
- **Booking the next load too late.** The best time to find a reload is while you are still loaded on the way in.
- **Forgetting accessorials.** Detention, lumper fees and layovers can change a loop's math. Make sure they are on the rate confirmation.
- **Not tracking results.** Without records, you cannot tell which lanes really pay.

## Round-trip planning checklist

- [ ] I know my current all-in cost per mile.
- [ ] I checked outbound loads from the delivery city before booking.
- [ ] I priced the full loop, including all deadhead.
- [ ] The loop fits my hours of service and appointment times.
- [ ] I have a backup option if the planned reload falls through.
- [ ] Accessorial terms are on the rate confirmation.
