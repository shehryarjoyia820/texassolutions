**Team driving** puts two drivers in one truck so the wheels keep turning while one of them sleeps. It can roughly double the miles a truck covers in a week, which is why expedited and long-haul shippers like teams. It also means living in a small space with another person for weeks at a time. Here is how it works, how the money splits and what makes teams last.

## How team driving works

One driver drives while the other rests in the sleeper berth or rides as a passenger. When the driver nears their limit, they swap. A well-run team can keep a truck moving most of the day, stopping mainly for fuel, inspections, loading and unloading.

Each driver:

- Holds their own license, medical card and qualifications
- Logs their own hours on the ELD under their own login
- Must follow their own driving, on-duty and weekly limits

## Hours of service for teams

The same basic limits apply to each team driver as to a solo driver: the 11-hour driving limit, the 14-hour window, the 30-minute break requirement and the 60/70-hour weekly limit. Our [hours of service rules guide](/blog/hours-of-service-rules-explained-11-14-70-hour.html) explains each one.

What makes teams different is how rest happens:

- **Sleeper berth splits.** Drivers can split their required off-duty time between the sleeper berth and another rest period in certain combinations. See [the sleeper berth split explained](/blog/sleeper-berth-split-explained.html).
- **Passenger seat time.** The rules include a team-specific provision that lets a limited amount of time in the passenger seat, right before or after a long sleeper period, count toward rest. Confirm how it works today before relying on it.
- **Swap timing.** The team has to plan swaps so neither driver runs out of hours at a bad place, such as in the middle of a city at rush hour or far from parking.

Rules can change, so confirm current details with FMCSA before building a schedule around them.

## A sample team schedule

Here is a simple schedule pattern many teams start with and then adjust:

| Time block | Driver A | Driver B |
|---|---|---|
| 6:00 am to 6:00 pm | On duty: up to 11 hours driving, plus break, fuel and inspection | Sleeper berth |
| 6:00 pm to 6:00 am | Sleeper berth | On duty: up to 11 hours driving, plus break, fuel and inspection |

Each driver works inside a 12-hour block, which fits within the 14-hour window, then gets 12 hours off, which covers the 10-hour reset. Watch the weekly 60/70-hour limit: 12-hour on-duty days use up 70 hours in under a week, so plan shorter shifts or a restart.

Real schedules shift with appointments, traffic and how well each driver sleeps in a moving truck. Some drivers sleep better at night, some do not mind day sleep. Match the shift to the person.

## What freight suits teams

Teams earn their premium on freight that values speed or long distances:

- **Expedited and time-critical loads**, where a solo driver legally cannot make the delivery time. See the [expedited freight guide](/blog/expedited-freight-guide.html).
- **Long cross-country lanes**, where non-stop driving saves a day or more.
- **High-value or security-sensitive loads**, where the shipper prefers the truck to stay attended.
- **Dedicated runs** with tight transit windows.

If your loads are short regional trips with long waits at docks, a team adds cost without adding much revenue.

## How team pay works

### Company teams

Many carriers pay a team rate per mile on all miles the truck runs, split between the two drivers, often 50/50. Others pay each driver only for the miles they personally drive. Ask which method applies and how detention, layovers and breakdowns are paid.

### Owner-operator teams

If you own the truck and run with a partner, you set the split. Common setups:

- **Spouses or partners** often treat it as one household income.
- **Owner plus hired co-driver**: the owner pays the co-driver per mile, a percentage, or a weekly amount. Treat this as hiring a driver, with full qualification and payroll or contractor decisions made with a CPA.
- **Two partners sharing ownership**: put it in a written agreement covering pay, expenses, who decides on loads and what happens if one person leaves.

## Hypothetical revenue example

These numbers are made up to show the math, not market rates.

- Solo truck: 2,500 miles per week at $2.30 per mile = **$5,750 gross**
- Team truck: 4,800 miles per week at $2.30 per mile = **$11,040 gross**

The team truck burns roughly twice the fuel and wears tires, brakes and the engine faster, and you are paying two people. Per-mile fixed costs (truck payment, insurance) drop because they are spread over more miles, but insurance often costs more with two drivers. Check the real result with the [cost per mile calculator](/cost-per-mile-calculator.html) before deciding.

## Choosing a team partner

Most teams fail because of the people, not the money. Before you commit:

- [ ] Run a short trial trip before a long contract
- [ ] Agree on driving style, speed and following distance
- [ ] Agree on cleanliness, food, smoking, music, phone calls and pets
- [ ] Set rules for who handles fuel, paperwork and broker calls
- [ ] Agree on home time and how long you stay out
- [ ] Plan how to resolve disagreements before they happen
- [ ] Confirm both drivers are approved by the insurer
- [ ] Put pay and expense splits in writing

Sleep is the biggest health issue. Invest in a good mattress, blackout curtains and earplugs, and make the cab quiet when one person is sleeping. A tired team is not safer than a solo driver.
