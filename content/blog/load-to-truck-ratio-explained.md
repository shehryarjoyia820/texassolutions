Spot rates go up and down for a simple reason: supply and demand. The **load-to-truck ratio** is one of the quickest ways to see that balance. It tells you whether a market has more freight than trucks, or more trucks than freight, and that helps you decide where to go and how hard to negotiate.

## What the load-to-truck ratio means

The ratio compares two counts from load board postings:

- **Loads:** freight posted by brokers and shippers looking for a truck.
- **Trucks:** capacity posted by carriers looking for a load.

The basic formula is:

**Load-to-truck ratio = posted loads / posted trucks**

The ratio is usually split by equipment type (dry van, reefer, flatbed and so on) and by area (region, state or metro market).

| Ratio | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Well above the market's normal | Tight capacity; carriers have more leverage |
| Around the market's normal | Balanced market |
| Well below the market's normal | Loose capacity; brokers have more leverage |

## Why it moves spot rates

When a broker has a load and few trucks are available, they have to raise the offer to get it covered. When many trucks are posted and waiting, the broker can hold the rate down because someone will take it.

That is why the ratio and spot rates tend to move in the same direction over time. The ratio is not a price, but it is a strong hint about which way prices are leaning.

## How to read it the right way

The ratio is useful, but only if you know its limits.

### Compare like with like

Each load board counts postings in its own way, and its users are not the whole market. A ratio from one source cannot be compared directly with a ratio from another. Stick to one source and compare:

- the same market over time (this week vs. last month), and
- different markets in the same week, for the same trailer type.

### Each trailer type has its own normal

Van, reefer and flatbed ratios sit at very different levels because carriers in each segment post trucks differently. A flatbed ratio that looks huge next to a van ratio may be perfectly normal for flatbed. Compare each type only with itself.

### It only sees the spot market

Contract freight, dedicated lanes and direct shipper loads are not posted on boards. The ratio shows spot market pressure, not the entire freight economy.

### Postings are not perfect

The same load can be posted by several brokers, and some posted loads are already covered. Trucks may stay posted after they book. Small markets can swing wildly from one day to the next. Look for trends over several days, not a single snapshot.

## How owner-operators use it

### 1. Pick where to deliver

Before you accept a load, check the ratio in the delivery market. A strong outbound ratio means a reload is more likely, often at a better rate. A weak one means you should ask more on the inbound load, or plan a longer reposition. Our guide on how to [plan a profitable round trip](/blog/plan-a-profitable-round-trip.html) walks through pricing the full loop.

### 2. Negotiate with confidence

If the ratio in the pickup market is high and climbing, the broker likely has fewer trucks to choose from. That is a good time to counter-offer. If the ratio is low, expect less room. See [how to negotiate freight rates](/blog/how-to-negotiate-freight-rates.html).

### 3. Time your home time

When ratios are low across your lanes, the truck earns less per day. That can be a sensible time for home time or maintenance. Our [freight market calendar](/blog/freight-market-calendar.html) shows the months when markets usually loosen.

### 4. Spot regional shifts

Seasonal freight, such as produce or holiday retail, shows up as rising ratios in certain regions before rates fully catch up. Watching those shifts can help you get the truck in position early.

## Worked example (hypothetical)

You are delivering a dry van load and choosing between two cities for your next pickup. You use one load board for both checks.

| Market | Van loads posted | Vans posted | Ratio | Its usual ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| City A | 1,200 | 300 | 4.0 | about 3.0 |
| City B | 900 | 450 | 2.0 | about 2.5 |

City A has a ratio above its normal level, so capacity looks tight. City B is below its normal level, so trucks are competing harder. All else being equal, City A is the better place to look for a reload, and the inbound load to City B should pay extra to make up for a weaker outbound market.

Before deciding, also check the deadhead from your delivery to each city and the rates actually being paid, then run the numbers in the [load profitability calculator](/load-profitability-calculator.html).

## Watch rates alongside the ratio

The ratio tells you which way the market is leaning. Posted and paid rates tell you where it actually is. Use both together:

- **Ratio rising, rates flat:** rates may follow soon, so hold firm on your counter-offers.
- **Ratio falling, rates still high:** the window may be closing, so book good loads while you can.
- **Ratio and rates both low:** focus on covering costs, shorter trips toward stronger markets, and steady broker relationships.

Neither number matters without your own cost per mile. A busy market only helps if the load pays more than it costs you to run it.

## Quick checklist before you trust the ratio

- [ ] Same data source for every comparison.
- [ ] Same equipment type.
- [ ] Compared to that market's recent history, not a fixed number.
- [ ] Looked at a trend over several days.
- [ ] Checked actual posted rates and deadhead, not just the ratio.
