**Buying a used semi truck** is how many owner-operators get started, and it can be a smart move if you buy the right truck at the right price. The risk is that you inherit someone else's expensive problems. This guide gives you a practical checklist, the records to ask for, red flags and a hypothetical budget so you buy with your eyes open.

## Start with what the truck needs to do

Before looking at listings, decide:

- **What freight will you haul?** Dry van, reefer, flatbed and power only have different needs. See [dry van vs reefer vs flatbed](/blog/dry-van-vs-reefer-vs-flatbed-which-trailer.html).
- **Where will you run?** Regional, OTR, mountains, or states with strict emissions rules.
- **What is your total budget?** Price plus taxes, registration, insurance, and a repair reserve.
- **Sleeper or day cab?** Long-haul work usually requires a sleeper.

A cheap truck that does not fit your freight or your lanes is not a good deal.

## Paperwork checks before you inspect

Do these first. If the paperwork fails, you do not need to drive across the state to see the truck.

1. **Title:** the seller's name matches, and the title is clean (not salvage or rebuilt unless you accept that).
2. **Liens:** confirm there is no outstanding loan, or that it will be paid off at sale with written proof.
3. **VIN match:** the VIN on the title matches the truck's door plate and frame.
4. **Maintenance records:** oil changes, major repairs, overhauls, aftertreatment work.
5. **Annual DOT inspection record:** the most recent periodic inspection report.
6. **Emissions compliance:** if you plan to run in states with special rules, confirm the engine qualifies.

A seller who cannot or will not provide records is telling you something.

## Used semi truck inspection checklist

Bring an independent heavy-truck mechanic you choose, not one the seller recommends. Have them check:

### Engine and drivetrain
- **ECM report:** fault codes, engine hours, idle time, total miles, hard braking events. Compare ECM miles with the odometer.
- **Oil analysis:** a lab sample can reveal coolant in the oil, fuel dilution or unusual metal wear.
- Blow-by, leaks, smoke on start-up and under load
- Turbo, injectors and cooling system condition
- Transmission shifting, clutch wear, differentials and driveline

### Emissions and aftertreatment
- DPF condition and regeneration history
- EGR and SCR/DEF system faults
- Any signs the emissions system was removed or tampered with. Deleted systems can create legal, inspection and resale problems; ask a qualified professional before buying any truck in that condition.

### Frame, suspension and body
- Frame cracks, welds, rust and alignment
- Air suspension bags, leaf springs, shocks and fifth wheel
- Cab mounts, doors, windows, sleeper condition

### Brakes, tires and electrical
- Brake linings, drums, air system leaks and pushrod travel
- Tire tread depth, matching, and age
- Lights, gauges, dash warnings and HVAC

### Test drive
- Drive loaded if possible, including a hill
- Watch temperature, oil pressure and boost
- Listen for noises at highway speed and while braking

Our [pre-trip inspection checklist](/blog/pre-trip-inspection-checklist.html) is a useful reminder of the items DOT inspectors look at too.

## Red flags that should make you slow down

- Seller refuses an independent inspection or ECM download
- ECM miles and odometer do not match
- Fresh paint on the frame or engine hiding leaks or welds
- No maintenance records at all
- Active fault codes cleared just before your visit
- Price far below similar trucks with no clear reason
- Pressure to send a deposit before you see the truck, especially by wire or gift card
- Title held in a different name or state with a story attached

Scams exist in truck sales. Never pay for a truck you have not verified in person or through a trusted inspector, and confirm ownership before money moves.

## Worked example: a realistic first-year budget

These are **hypothetical** numbers to show how to plan, not real market prices.

| Item | Hypothetical amount |
|---|---|
| Purchase price | $60,000 |
| Taxes, title and registration | $5,000 |
| Pre-purchase inspection and oil analysis | $600 |
| Immediate repairs found in inspection | $4,000 |
| Repair reserve for the first year | $10,000 |
| **Total to plan for** | **$79,600** |

The truck "costs" $60,000, but the money you actually need is close to $80,000. Many new owner-operators budget only for the price and get stuck after the first major repair. ## After you buy

- Change all fluids and filters unless records show it was just done
- Start your own maintenance log on day one
- Set a monthly amount aside for repairs and do not touch it
- Get your insurance, registration and IFTA set up before the first load

The first few months tell you a lot about the truck. Watch fuel economy, oil consumption and any new fault codes closely, and fix small problems before they grow into roadside breakdowns that cost you loads as well as repair bills.
