Driver Qualification File Checklist for Small Carriers

By Texas Solutions Dispatch Team · October 21, 2026 · 5 min read

Compliance Driver qualification
Quick answer

What goes in a driver qualification file?

A driver qualification (DQ) file holds the documents that prove a driver is qualified to operate a commercial motor vehicle: the employment application, motor vehicle records, previous employer safety history, road test or CDL equivalent, medical certificate with examiner verification, and annual reviews. Interstate carriers must keep one for every driver, including an owner-operator who drives for their own authority. Confirm the current list in 49 CFR Part 391 on the FMCSA website.

A driver qualification file checklist keeps you from finding out during an audit that one missing form makes a driver unqualified on paper. Federal rules require interstate carriers to keep a file for every driver, and that includes the owner-operator who drives under their own MC number. Here is what goes in the file, when each item gets updated, and the gaps that cause trouble most often.

Who needs a DQ file

The driver qualification rules in 49 CFR Part 391 apply to drivers of commercial motor vehicles in interstate commerce. In general that means vehicles with a gross weight or weight rating over 10,001 pounds, vehicles placarded for hazmat, and certain passenger vehicles. A CDL is not the trigger: a non-CDL hotshot or box truck over the weight threshold usually still needs a qualified driver and a DQ file.

If you are a one-truck carrier, you keep the file on yourself. Many new carriers miss this, then struggle during the new entrant safety audit because the "company" never qualified its own driver.

Intrastate rules vary by state. Texas and most other states adopt much of Part 391, but with differences, so check your state rules if you run only in-state.

The DQ file checklist

Use this list for each driver. Check the current text of 49 CFR 391.51 on the FMCSA website before you rely on it, because rules get updated.

Before the driver's first dispatch

  • [ ] Employment application with the information required by 391.21, signed and dated by the driver. It must list employers for the past three years, plus any commercial driving jobs going back further as the rule requires.
  • [ ] Motor vehicle record (MVR) from every state where the driver held a license or permit in the past three years, requested within 30 days of the hire date.
  • [ ] Safety performance history request sent to each DOT-regulated employer the driver worked for in the past three years, plus the responses or a record of your good-faith efforts to get them.
  • [ ] Road test certificate, or a copy of a valid CDL (or an equivalent document the rules accept) in place of the road test.
  • [ ] Medical examiner's certificate (the "medical card") that is current.
  • [ ] Proof that the medical examiner is on the National Registry of Certified Medical Examiners, such as a dated printout or note of your verification.
  • [ ] Clearinghouse pre-employment full query completed and clear for CDL drivers. The query record is kept with drug and alcohol records, but note in your hiring checklist that it was done. See the drug and alcohol testing program guide.
  • [ ] Pre-employment drug test with a verified negative result before any safety-sensitive work, for CDL drivers. Again, file the result in the separate testing file.

Every year

  • [ ] Annual MVR from each state where the driver holds a license or permit.
  • [ ] Annual review of the driving record, signed by the person who did it, noting whether the driver still meets minimum requirements.
  • [ ] Annual Clearinghouse limited query (or full query) for CDL drivers.

As needed

  • [ ] New medical certificate every time the old one expires. Exams can be valid for up to 24 months but are often shorter. Read the medical card and DOT physical guide.
  • [ ] Updated CDL copy after renewal, upgrade or endorsement change.
  • [ ] Skill performance evaluation certificate or medical exemption letter, if the driver relies on one.

A simple filing system that passes an audit

You do not need software to stay organized, but you do need consistency. A setup that works for many small fleets:

  1. One folder per driver, paper or digital, with the same section order every time: application, MVRs, previous employer history, road test or CDL, medical, annual reviews.
  2. A separate locked folder for drug and alcohol records and Clearinghouse queries.
  3. A tracking sheet with one row per driver and columns for medical card expiration, CDL expiration, annual MVR due date and annual Clearinghouse query due date.
  4. Calendar reminders 30 days before each expiration.
  5. A quarterly self-check where you open every file and compare it against the checklist above.

Digital files are generally acceptable as long as you can produce them promptly when asked. Keep backups.

Common DQ file mistakes

These are the problems that show up again and again:

  • Expired medical card with no new one on file. Driving with an expired medical certificate is treated as using an unqualified driver.
  • No National Registry verification for the examiner. The card alone is not enough.
  • Missing previous employer inquiries, or inquiries sent but no follow-up documented. Write down every attempt, with dates.
  • MVR pulled from only one state when the driver held licenses in more than one during the three-year window.
  • Annual review not signed or dated.
  • Application missing required employment history, especially gaps that are not explained.
  • Testing records mixed into the DQ file, which creates a confidentiality problem.

How long to keep it

The general rule: keep the DQ file for the length of employment plus three years after the driver leaves. Certain recurring documents, such as MVRs, annual reviews, and medical certificates, may be removed from the file three years after the date they were executed. Drug and alcohol records follow their own retention schedule under Parts 382 and 40. When in doubt, keep it, and confirm the current periods with FMCSA.

Worked example: a two-truck carrier

Say you run your own truck and just hired one driver. Your file workload looks like this:

Task For you For your driver How often
MVR review 1 1 Yearly
Clearinghouse query 1 1 Yearly
Medical card check 1 1 At each expiration
Full new-hire file Already done 1 Once

That is about four routine tasks a year plus medical renewals. It takes a few hours, which is far less time than a failed audit or an insurance claim denied because a driver was not qualified on paper. Frequently asked questions

Do I need a DQ file if I am the only driver in my company?

Yes. If you hold your own authority and drive, you are both the motor carrier and the driver, so you must keep a DQ file on yourself. Auditors check it the same way they check a fleet driver's file.

How long do I keep a driver qualification file?

The general rule is to keep the file for as long as the driver works for you plus three years after they leave. Some recurring items, such as older annual reviews and MVRs, can be removed once they are more than three years old. Check 49 CFR 391.51 for the details.

Are drug test results kept in the DQ file?

No. Drug and alcohol testing records belong in a separate, confidential file with restricted access. Keep them apart from the DQ file, which a wider group of staff may handle.

Does a box truck or hotshot driver need a DQ file?

If the vehicle is a commercial motor vehicle used in interstate commerce, which generally starts at a gross weight or rating over 10,001 pounds, driver qualification rules apply even when no CDL is needed. Confirm how the rules apply to your setup with FMCSA.
