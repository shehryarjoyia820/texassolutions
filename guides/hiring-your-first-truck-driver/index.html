Hiring Your First Truck Driver: A Step-by-Step Guide

By Texas Solutions Dispatch Team · October 21, 2026 · 5 min read

Drivers Small fleet growth
Quick answer

How do I hire my first truck driver as an owner-operator?

Run the numbers first to confirm a second truck will earn enough to pay a driver, then get the driver approved by your insurance agent, complete the DQ file, pre-employment drug test and Clearinghouse query, and agree on a written pay plan. Decide on employee versus contractor status with a CPA or attorney before the first paycheck. Confirm current FMCSA qualification rules before the driver's first load.

Hiring your first truck driver is the moment an owner-operator becomes a fleet owner. It can double your revenue, but it also doubles your compliance, insurance and cash flow exposure, and you are now responsible for someone else's driving. This guide walks through the decision and the hiring steps in the order that keeps you out of trouble.

Before you hire

Step 1: Make sure the numbers work

Before you post a job, confirm a second truck can pay a driver and still make money for you.

Hypothetical example (made-up numbers for illustration):

Item Per week
Miles 2,500
Gross revenue at $2.40/mile $6,000
Fuel $1,900
Truck and trailer payment $700
Insurance for the added unit $400
Maintenance and tires reserve $375
Other (ELD, permits, tolls, dispatch, admin) $500
Before driver pay $2,125
Driver pay at $0.60/mile $1,500
Payroll taxes and benefits (estimate) $200
Left for you $425

In this example the second truck makes money, but a slow week, one repair or a low-paying lane can wipe it out. Run your own numbers with the driver pay calculator, and check your full cost per mile and break-even point before committing. Keep a cash reserve covering several weeks of expenses, because you pay the driver before some brokers pay you.

Step 2: Decide employee or contractor

This is a legal and tax question, not a preference. Generally, if you control when, where and how the driver works, provide the truck and set the rules, the driver may be an employee under IRS and state tests. Some states apply stricter tests than others.

Hiring a company driver as a W-2 employee means payroll, withholding, unemployment tax and workers' compensation where required. Leasing on an owner-operator who brings their own truck is a different arrangement with its own federal leasing rules.

Talk to a CPA or employment attorney before the first paycheck. If you might work with leased owner-operators later, ask about that arrangement too.

Step 3: Get insurance approval first

Call your insurance agent before you interview seriously. Ask:

  • What are the minimum age and experience requirements for an added driver?
  • What will adding a driver and unit do to my premium?
  • Which violations or accidents on an MVR would disqualify a candidate?
  • Do I need occupational accident or workers' compensation coverage?

Many insurers want a few years of verifiable experience. Finding out after you have made an offer is a costly mistake. Get the answers in writing.

Finding and qualifying the driver

Step 4: Find and screen candidates

Write a job post that is honest about the lane, home time, equipment and pay plan. Then screen in stages:

  1. Phone screen: experience, equipment type, home time expectations, any recent tickets or accidents.
  2. Application: the full DOT-compliant employment application.
  3. MVR and insurance check: send the MVR to your agent for approval.
  4. Interview: ask how they plan a trip, handle a late appointment, or deal with a broker who will not pay detention. Listen for communication skills.
  5. Road test (or accept a valid CDL in place of it, where the rules allow), plus a ride-along if you can.
  6. References from previous drivers or dispatchers, not just HR offices.

Step 5: Qualify the driver before the first load

Nothing moves until these are done:

  • [ ] Signed employment application
  • [ ] MVRs from every state licensed in the past three years
  • [ ] Previous employer safety performance history requests sent
  • [ ] Road test certificate or CDL copy
  • [ ] Current medical card and National Registry verification
  • [ ] Clearinghouse pre-employment full query with driver consent
  • [ ] Negative pre-employment drug test result received
  • [ ] Driver added to your random testing pool
  • [ ] Insurance approval in writing
  • [ ] Driver added to your ELD account

Use the driver qualification file checklist and the drug and alcohol testing program guide for details. Confirm current requirements with FMCSA.

Pay and onboarding

Step 6: Choose a pay plan and put it in writing

Common structures:

  • Per mile: simple and common. Decide whether you pay practical, loaded or all miles.
  • Percentage of load revenue: rewards good rates but varies week to week.
  • Hourly or daily: common for local and regional work.
  • Weekly salary: steady for the driver, risky for you in slow weeks.

Whatever you choose, write down extra pay for detention, layovers, stop-offs, breakdowns and tarping if it applies. Explain how and when you pay, and any deductions. Clear rules prevent most driver disputes.

Step 7: Onboard properly

First impressions shape retention. A simple orientation covers:

  • Company policies: speed, idling, passengers, pets, personal conveyance
  • Truck walkaround and pre-trip inspection expectations
  • ELD login, logs and hours of service
  • Fuel card limits and where to fuel
  • Paperwork: bills of lading, PODs, lumper receipts and how to send them
  • Who to call for breakdowns, accidents and load problems, day or night

Ride with the driver for the first day or two if you can.

Step 8: Check in and keep good drivers

The first 90 days decide whether a driver stays. Set a short call at the end of week one, week four and month three. Ask what is working, what is frustrating and whether pay settlements are clear. Fix small problems fast: a late paycheck or a truck that keeps breaking down will cost you a good driver sooner than a slightly lower rate per mile.

Keep the recurring compliance tasks on a calendar too: medical card expiration, CDL renewal, the annual MVR review and the annual Clearinghouse query. Missing one of these can make a good driver unqualified on paper overnight.

Mistakes first-time fleet owners make

  • Hiring before insurance approval
  • Letting a driver run "just one load" before the drug test result comes back
  • No written pay agreement
  • Not tracking medical card and license expiration dates
  • Handing a driver your worst lanes and expecting them to stay

Fix these early and growing beyond two trucks gets much easier.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I pay my first driver?

Pay depends on your lane, freight type, home time and local market. Set a pay plan you can afford at your realistic revenue per mile, then compare it to what drivers in your area are offered. The driver pay calculator can help you test per-mile, percentage and weekly options.

Can I hire a driver as a 1099 contractor?

Sometimes, but classification depends on how much control you have over the work, and federal and state tests differ. Misclassification can lead to back taxes and penalties, so talk to a CPA or employment attorney before deciding.

Will my insurance cover a new driver automatically?

Usually not. Most commercial auto policies require you to submit each driver for approval, and insurers often have minimum age and experience requirements. Get approval in writing before the driver touches the truck.

Where do small carriers find good drivers?

Referrals from drivers you trust, local CDL schools for experienced instructors or recent grads with ride-along time, online job boards, and trucking groups are common sources. Small carriers often win on home time, respect and consistent equipment rather than top pay.
