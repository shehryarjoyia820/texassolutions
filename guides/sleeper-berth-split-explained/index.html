Sleeper Berth Split Explained: 7/3 and 8/2 With Examples

By Texas Solutions Dispatch Team · October 21, 2026 · 5 min read

Compliance Hours of service
Quick answer

How does the split sleeper berth rule work?

Property-carrying drivers can split their required 10 hours off duty into two periods: one of at least 7 hours in the sleeper berth and one of at least 2 hours off duty or in the sleeper, totaling at least 10 hours. When the two periods are paired, neither counts against the 14-hour window, and you recalculate your hours from the end of the first period. Confirm current rules with FMCSA.

The sleeper berth split lets a property-carrying driver break the required 10 hours off duty into two pieces instead of one long block. Used well, it helps you wait out a late appointment, dodge rush hour or nap through the hottest part of the day without wasting your 14-hour window.

It is also the hours of service rule drivers most often get wrong. This guide walks through how the split works under the federal rules as we understand them, with a worked example. Rules can change, so confirm the current version with FMCSA before you rely on it.

The basic rule

Under the federal hours of service rules for property-carrying drivers, you normally need 10 consecutive hours off duty before you can drive again. The sleeper berth provision gives you an alternative:

  • One period of at least 7 consecutive hours in the sleeper berth, and
  • One period of at least 2 consecutive hours off duty, in the sleeper berth, or a combination of both, and
  • The two periods together must total at least 10 hours.

That gives you two practical combinations: 7/3 and 8/2. (A longer long period, such as 7.5/2.5, also works as long as the long period is in the sleeper and the total reaches 10.)

When two qualifying periods are paired, neither period counts against your 14-hour window. That is the real value of the split. The periods can be taken in either order.

Passenger-carrying drivers have different sleeper berth rules, so this guide only covers property carriers.

How to recalculate your hours

This is the part that confuses people. After you complete the second period of a qualifying pair, you recalculate your available hours starting from the end of the first period of the pair.

In plain terms:

  1. Find the end of the first qualifying rest period.
  2. Count the driving time since then. Subtract it from 11 to get the driving time you have left.
  3. Count the elapsed time since then, but leave out the second rest period. Subtract it from 14 to get what is left of your window.

Each new qualifying period can then pair with the one before it, so a driver can keep "leapfrogging" through the week. Your 60/70-hour limit still applies the whole time.

Worked example: a 7/3 split

Here is a hypothetical day. Times are on a 24-hour clock.

Time Status Notes
06:00 - 12:00 Driving 6 hours driving
12:00 - 15:00 Off duty 3 hours, the short period (also covers the 30-minute break)
15:00 - 19:00 Driving 4 more hours; total driving 10, elapsed 13 since 06:00
19:00 - 02:00 Sleeper berth 7 hours, the long period

At 02:00 the 3-hour and 7-hour periods pair up (3 + 7 = 10). Now recalculate from the end of the first period, which was 15:00:

  • Driving since 15:00: 4 hours. 11 - 4 = 7 hours of driving left.
  • Window since 15:00, leaving out the 7-hour sleeper period: 15:00 to 19:00 = 4 hours. 14 - 4 = 10 hours of window left.

So at 02:00 the driver can drive up to 7 hours, all within a window that runs until 12:00.

Say the driver drives 02:00 to 09:00 (7 hours), then takes 3 hours off duty from 09:00 to 12:00. That 3-hour period pairs with the previous 7-hour sleeper period. Recalculate from the end of the 7-hour period (02:00): 7 hours driven, so 4 hours of driving left, and the window used is 7 hours, so 7 hours of window left. The leapfrog continues.

Notice what made the example work: on the first day, the driver stayed inside the original 14-hour window (06:00 to 20:00) while driving. Do not plan to drive past your 14th hour on the assumption that a future break will fix it. If the second period never happens, the time is a violation.

The 30-minute break and the split

The federal 30-minute break applies after 8 cumulative hours of driving without at least a 30-minute interruption. Under current rules the interruption can be on duty not driving, off duty or sleeper berth time. Both periods of a split are long enough to count as that break. Short-haul drivers using certain exceptions may not need the 30-minute break at all. Check FMCSA for the current wording.

When the split helps, and when it hurts

The split is a planning tool. It helps when:

  • A receiver will not take you until a set time. Spend the wait in the sleeper as part of a qualifying period instead of burning your window in a parking lot.
  • You want to avoid city traffic. Park for 2 or 3 hours during rush hour and come back with your window preserved.
  • Detention eats your day. If you know you will sit for hours, logging part of it as a qualifying period can protect your window. Be honest: if you are on duty, such as being required to stay with the truck and move it, log it that way.

It can hurt when:

  • You end up short on sleep because the long period was broken up or noisy.
  • Your ELD and your own math disagree. Most ELDs calculate splits automatically, but double-check the clocks before you roll.
  • You stack splits all week and lose track of your 70-hour total.

Quick checklist before you use a split

  • [ ] Long period is at least 7 consecutive hours, logged as sleeper berth
  • [ ] Short period is at least 2 consecutive hours, off duty or sleeper
  • [ ] Both periods total at least 10 hours
  • [ ] You stay within your current 11 and 14 hours until the pair is complete
  • [ ] Your 60/70-hour cycle has room
  • [ ] Your ELD shows the recalculated clocks you expect

Frequently asked questions

Can I split my break 5/5 or 6/4?

Not under the current federal rule for property carriers. The long period must be at least 7 hours in the sleeper berth, and the two periods must add up to at least 10 hours, so 7/3 and 8/2 are the common splits.

Does the order of the two periods matter?

No. You can take the short period first or the long period first. What matters is that the two qualifying periods pair up and total at least 10 hours.

Does the 2-hour period have to be in the sleeper berth?

No. The shorter period can be off duty, in the sleeper berth or a combination of the two. The longer period of at least 7 hours must be in the sleeper berth.

Does a split sleeper reset my 70-hour clock?

No. The sleeper berth split only affects your daily 11-hour and 14-hour limits. Your 60/70-hour cycle keeps counting until you take a 34-hour restart, if your carrier uses one.
