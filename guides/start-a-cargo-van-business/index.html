How to Start a Cargo Van Business: A Practical Guide

By Texas Solutions Dispatch Team · October 21, 2026 · 5 min read

Business Cargo van
Quick answer

How do you start a cargo van business?

Pick a freight model first (local courier, contract routes or long-distance expedite), then buy a van that fits it, form a business entity, get the right registrations and commercial insurance, and track your cost per mile before you take rates. Interstate for-hire hauling generally needs FMCSA operating authority, so confirm your exact requirements with FMCSA and your state before you start.

A cargo van business is one of the lowest-cost ways to get into freight. You can start with one van, a regular driver's license and a modest budget, and haul small, time-sensitive loads that bigger trucks cannot do economically. The catch is that margins are thin, so the business only works if you plan it like a real carrier from day one.

Choose your freight model first

Before you shop for a van, decide what kind of work you want. Each model needs different equipment, hours and customers.

  • Local courier and same-day delivery. Short runs for medical labs, parts suppliers, print shops and offices. Steady if you build relationships, but many small jobs.
  • Contract routes. Recurring routes for a logistics company or retailer. Predictable income, less freedom on rates.
  • Long-distance expedite. One shipment, one van, driven straight through to the receiver. Higher pay per load, more time on the road and more empty miles coming back. See our expedited freight guide.
  • Final mile. Delivering goods to homes and businesses, sometimes with assembly or inside delivery. A growing niche with its own customers and equipment needs.

Pick one to start. Trying to do everything at once usually means you are good at nothing.

Pick the right van

The van should match the freight, not the other way around. Questions to ask before you buy:

  • Cargo length and height. Can it take a standard pallet or two? A high-roof extended van gives more room than a standard roof.
  • Payload. Check the manufacturer's payload rating, not just the cargo space. Overloading hurts brakes, tires and your insurance claim.
  • Fuel economy and engine. You will put a lot of miles on it. Compare real fuel use and maintenance costs, not just purchase price.
  • Doors and floor. Side and rear doors, a good floor and tie-down points (E-track or D-rings) make loading faster and safer.
  • Condition and history if buying used. Get a pre-purchase inspection.

Keep the purchase price realistic. A van with a big payment can sink the business before you build a customer base.

Set up the business and paperwork

Here is a typical sequence. Requirements change, so confirm current rules with FMCSA, the IRS and your state.

  1. Form a business entity (many owners choose an LLC) and get an EIN from the IRS. Talk to a CPA or attorney about which structure fits you.
  2. Open a separate business bank account. Keep every dollar of van income and expense in it.
  3. Get the right registrations. If you haul for-hire across state lines, you generally need a USDOT number and FMCSA operating authority (MC number), plus a process agent filing (BOC-3) and UCR registration. In-state-only work follows your state's rules.
  4. Buy commercial insurance. Brokers and shippers commonly ask for auto liability and cargo coverage. A personal auto policy usually will not cover commercial hauling. Ask your insurance agent exactly what limits your customers require.
  5. Set up bookkeeping from the first day so you can track cost per mile and taxes.

If you want help with the filings, our trucking authority setup service walks through the steps with you.

What about hours of service and medical cards?

Many federal rules for commercial vehicles are tied to vehicle weight, and most cargo vans fall under the main weight thresholds. That can mean fewer federal requirements than a heavier truck. But state rules, hazmat loads and customer contracts can add requirements, so confirm with FMCSA and your state rather than assuming you are exempt.

Know your cost per mile before you quote

Van rates per mile are lower than for semis, so small cost mistakes matter a lot. Here is a simple hypothetical example for one month.

Cost item (hypothetical) Monthly
Van payment $900
Insurance $600
Fuel (8,000 miles at 16 mpg, $3.50/gal) $1,750
Maintenance and tires reserve $480
Phone, software, load board, misc. $250
Total $3,980

That works out to about $0.50 per mile on 8,000 miles, before paying yourself. If a load pays $1.20 per loaded mile but you drive back empty the same distance, you only earn $0.60 per total mile. That is barely above cost. Run your own numbers with the cost per mile calculator, and always count the empty miles home.

Find freight and customers

Cargo van freight comes from several places:

  • Expedite and van-specific load boards used by brokers for small shipments.
  • Direct local customers such as labs, print shops, parts stores and contractors. Walk in, call, and follow up.
  • Contract routes with delivery and logistics companies.
  • Brokers who specialize in expedite and need reliable vans on short notice.

Reliability is your product. Answer the phone fast, show up on time, send pictures of signed paperwork right away, and your best customers will call you before they post the load anywhere.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Buying a van before you know what freight you will haul.
  • Using personal insurance for commercial work.
  • Accepting long one-way runs without a plan to get home loaded.
  • Mixing personal and business money.
  • Ignoring maintenance until something breaks on the road.

When to move up to a box truck

Many cargo van owners grow into a box truck once they keep turning down freight that will not fit. A box truck can carry more pallets, often has a liftgate, and opens up different loads and customers. It also brings more rules: depending on weight, you may need a DOT medical card, hours-of-service compliance and an ELD. Read box truck vs cargo van before you make the jump.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need an MC number for a cargo van?

If you haul other people's freight for pay across state lines, you generally need FMCSA operating authority (an MC number) even with a light van. Purely local, in-state work follows state rules, so check with FMCSA and your state before you book anything.

Do I need a CDL to drive a cargo van?

No. Cargo vans are well under the weight where a CDL applies, so a regular driver's license usually works. Your insurance company may still set its own age and driving-record requirements.

Is a cargo van business profitable?

It can be when costs stay low and the van stays busy, but rates per mile are lower than for larger trucks and loads are small. Profit depends on your lanes, your deadhead and how well you control costs, so run your numbers before you buy.

When should I move from a cargo van to a box truck?

Consider it when you regularly turn down freight because it will not fit, when customers ask for liftgate or pallet capacity, or when your van runs at full capacity most weeks. Make sure you understand the extra rules that come with a heavier vehicle first.
