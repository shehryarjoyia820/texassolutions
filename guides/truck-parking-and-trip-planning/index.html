Truck Parking and Trip Planning: How to Never Get Stuck

By Texas Solutions Dispatch Team · October 21, 2026 · 5 min read

Drivers Trip planning
Quick answer

How do truck drivers plan trips so they can always find parking?

Work backward from your hours of service: figure out where your 11 hours of driving or 14-hour window will run out, then pick a primary parking spot well before that point plus one or two backups. Aim to park before evening when lots fill up, use reserved parking when the stakes are high, and ask shippers and receivers about overnight parking ahead of time.

Finding a safe place to park is one of the hardest parts of the job, and poor truck parking planning can cost you hours, sleep and even a violation. Drivers who rarely get stuck usually are not lucky. They plan where they will stop before they leave.

This guide gives you a simple trip planning method built around hours of service, a worked example and a checklist you can use on every load.

Why parking planning matters

When you cannot find a spot, every option is bad:

  • You keep driving while tired, looking for space.
  • You run out of hours and risk a violation.
  • You park somewhere unsafe or illegal, like a ramp or an empty lot with no lighting.
  • You use up your 14-hour window circling, which cuts into tomorrow's miles.

Good planning protects your safety, your logbook and your income. It also makes it easier to hit appointments, which keeps brokers calling you back.

Step-by-step trip planning method

1. Start with your clocks

Before you plan miles, know your available time: driving hours left, time left in your 14-hour window, when your 30-minute break is due and how much room you have on your 60/70-hour cycle. The hours of service calculator helps you map them out.

2. Estimate realistic driving speed

Do not plan with the speed limit. Plan with an average that includes traffic, construction, fuel stops, weather and terrain. Many drivers use a conservative average well below the posted limit for planning, then adjust from experience on that lane.

3. Find your "must stop" point

Multiply your available driving hours by your realistic average speed. That tells you roughly how far you can go. Then check that you also fit inside your 14-hour window, including loading, fueling and breaks.

4. Pick a primary spot before that point

Choose a parking spot about an hour before you run out, not right at the edge. That cushion covers traffic jams and full lots.

5. Pick one or two backups

List backups both before and after your primary spot, if hours allow. A backup 30 to 60 minutes earlier is often more useful than one farther ahead.

6. Plan truck-legal routes

Use truck-specific routing that accounts for low clearances, weight-restricted bridges, restricted routes and hazmat restrictions if you haul hazmat. Consumer navigation apps can send a truck down roads it should not be on.

7. Check fuel and weather

Plan fuel stops so you are not forced to stop at a crowded location at the wrong time. Check weather along the route, especially in winter. See our winter driving and chain laws guide.

Worked example

Here is a hypothetical day for a driver hauling a dry van load 640 miles:

  • Driver comes on duty at 05:00 after a full 10-hour break.
  • Pre-trip and paperwork: 30 minutes. Driving starts at 05:30.
  • Planning average: 52 mph including traffic and stops.
  • 11 hours of driving x 52 mph = about 572 miles possible today.
  • The 14-hour window ends at 19:00.

The load is 640 miles, so the driver will not deliver today. The plan:

Item Time
Drive 05:30 - 10:30 about 260 miles
30-minute break and fuel 10:30 - 11:00
Drive 11:00 - 16:30 about 285 miles, 545 total
Park at primary spot 16:30, with 30 minutes of driving and 2.5 hours of window left
Backup spot about 40 miles earlier, if the primary is full

The driver parks around 545 miles in, ahead of the evening rush for spaces, with 95 miles left for the morning. That leaves an easy early-morning delivery and keeps hours in reserve for the next load. Stopping slightly early here cost little, while pushing to the last minute could have meant hunting for parking at dark with no hours left.

Where to park

Common options, each with tradeoffs:

  • Truck stops and travel centers. Fuel, food and showers, but popular ones fill up in the evening. Some offer paid reserved spaces you can book ahead.
  • Public rest areas. Free and convenient, but spaces are limited and time limits vary by state.
  • Shipper and receiver lots. Some allow overnight parking before an appointment or after unloading. Ask at booking, not at 9 p.m. Get permission in writing if possible.
  • Truck-friendly businesses. Some retailers and businesses allow trucks, others ban them or tow. Always confirm with the manager and look for posted signs.
  • Secure paid lots. Fenced, lit lots near big metro areas. Useful for high-value loads or long resets.

Avoid ramps, shoulders and unlit empty lots. Besides the legal risk, they are dangerous for you and other drivers.

Parking and the 14-hour clock

Delays at shippers are the most common reason drivers end up short on time and parking. Before you accept a load, ask about facility hours, average wait times and whether they allow overnight parking. If a facility holds you past your appointment, document the times so you can request detention. Our guide on how to get detention paid explains how. When you get kicked out after unloading with no hours left, personal conveyance may apply. See personal conveyance and yard moves for when it does and does not.

Daily trip planning checklist

  • [ ] Clocks checked: 11, 14, 30-minute break, 60/70 cycle
  • [ ] Realistic average speed chosen for this lane
  • [ ] Truck-legal route confirmed
  • [ ] Fuel stops planned
  • [ ] Weather checked along the full route
  • [ ] Primary parking spot about an hour before hours run out
  • [ ] One or two backup spots identified
  • [ ] Shipper/receiver parking rules and hours confirmed
  • [ ] Reserved parking booked if arriving late in a busy area

Frequently asked questions

Can I park on a highway ramp or shoulder if I run out of hours?

Many states prohibit or restrict parking on ramps and shoulders except in emergencies, and it is risky for you and other drivers. Plan to stop earlier instead, and check the rules in each state you run.

How long can I park at a public rest area?

Time limits vary by state and location and are usually posted at the entrance. Some allow a full rest period, others set shorter limits. Read the signs every time.

Is paid reserved parking worth it?

It can be when you will arrive late in a busy area, you are hauling high-value freight, or the next morning's appointment is close by. Compare the cost to the risk of losing hours searching for a spot.
