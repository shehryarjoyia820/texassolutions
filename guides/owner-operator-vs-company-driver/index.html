Owner-Operator vs Company Driver: Which Path Is Right for You?

By Texas Solutions Dispatch Team · October 21, 2026 · 4 min read

Business Drivers
Quick answer

Is it better to be an owner-operator or a company driver?

A company driver earns a steadier paycheck with fewer costs and less risk, while an owner-operator can earn more but pays for the truck, fuel, insurance and repairs and carries all the business risk. Becoming an owner-operator makes sense when you have savings, know your cost per mile and want to run a business, not just drive.

The choice between owner-operator vs company driver is one of the biggest decisions a truck driver makes. Running your own truck promises more money and more control, but it also turns you into a business owner with real costs and real risk. This guide compares both paths honestly so you can pick the one that fits your finances, skills and goals.

The basic difference

  • Company driver: you drive the carrier's truck, the carrier pays all operating costs, and you get a paycheck, usually per mile, per hour, per load or a percentage. You are typically a W-2 employee.
  • Owner-operator: you own or lease the truck and pay for fuel, insurance, maintenance, tolls and more. You earn the revenue from loads, and what is left after costs is your income. You are self-employed.

Owner-operators either lease on to a carrier and run under its authority, or run under their own authority (their own MC number) and work directly with brokers and shippers.

What owner-operators really earn

The big gross numbers can be misleading. Here is a hypothetical week to show the difference between revenue and income.

  • Revenue: 2,500 miles x $2.40 = $6,000
  • Fuel (2,500 miles / 6.5 mpg x $3.90): about $1,500
  • Truck payment: $600
  • Insurance: $350
  • Maintenance and tire reserve: $400
  • Dispatch, ELD, permits and other costs: $500
  • Total costs: about $3,350
  • Left before taxes: about $2,650

From that, the owner-operator still pays income and self-employment taxes, health insurance and saves for slow weeks. A company driver earning, say, $1,500 to $1,800 a week in this hypothetical has fewer worries, but less upside. Learn more in how much owner-operators make and run your own numbers with the cost per mile calculator.

Advantages of each path

Company driver

  • Steady, predictable pay
  • No truck payment or repair bills
  • Benefits may be included
  • Less paperwork and fewer business decisions
  • Easier to change jobs if a company is a bad fit
  • Time to learn the industry before taking on risk

Owner-operator

  • Higher earning potential when rates and costs are managed well
  • Control over which loads you take and where you run
  • Ability to build equity in a truck and a business
  • Business expenses may be deductible (ask a CPA)
  • Option to grow into a small fleet

The risks of going independent

Owner-operators fail most often for reasons they could have planned for:

  • Not knowing cost per mile and taking loads that lose money
  • Too little cash reserve for a major repair or a slow month
  • Overpriced trucks with payments the business cannot support
  • Waiting to get paid for 30 days or more without a plan for cash flow
  • Underestimating paperwork like IFTA, 2290 and compliance

None of these are reasons not to do it. They are reasons to prepare.

Signs you are ready to become an owner-operator

Use this checklist before you make the move:

  • [ ] At least a couple of years of safe driving experience
  • [ ] A clean driving record and inspection history
  • [ ] Savings for a down payment and several months of costs
  • [ ] A written budget with your expected cost per mile
  • [ ] A plan for freight: lease on, own authority with a dispatcher, or direct customers
  • [ ] Basic bookkeeping skills or a bookkeeper lined up
  • [ ] A clear understanding of the insurance you will need

If several boxes are unchecked, staying a company driver for another year while you save and learn can be the smarter business decision. Our guide on how to become an owner-operator covers the steps in detail.

Lease on or get your own authority?

Leasing on to a carrier is a middle step: you own the truck, but the carrier provides authority, insurance options and often freight. It is simpler but you give up some control and pay the carrier a share. Getting your own authority gives you full control but means more setup and compliance. Some new carriers also explore MC lease options as they build their own track record. Talk to an attorney about any lease agreement before you sign.

Frequently asked questions

Do owner-operators make more than company drivers?

Gross revenue is much higher, but owner-operators pay their own operating costs out of it. Net income can be higher or lower than a company driver's pay depending on rates, miles, costs and how well the business is managed.

What is the difference between a leased owner-operator and one with their own authority?

A leased owner-operator runs their own truck under another carrier's authority and usually gets freight and some support from that carrier. An owner-operator with their own authority is the motor carrier, finds their own freight or uses a dispatcher, and handles all compliance.

How much money should I save before becoming an owner-operator?

Enough to cover your down payment, insurance down payment, startup fees and at least a few months of operating costs while you wait to be paid. The right amount depends on your truck and situation, so build a budget before you commit.

How are taxes different for owner-operators?

Company drivers are usually W-2 employees with taxes withheld. Owner-operators are self-employed, pay estimated taxes and self-employment tax, and can deduct business expenses. Talk to a CPA who works with truckers.
