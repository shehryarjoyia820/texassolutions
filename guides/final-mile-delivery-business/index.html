How to Start a Final Mile Delivery Business

By Texas Solutions Dispatch Team · October 21, 2026 · 4 min read

Business Final mile
Quick answer

How does a final mile delivery business work?

A final mile business delivers goods on the last leg from a warehouse or hub to a home or business, often with a box truck and a two-person crew. Most carriers work under contracts with logistics companies or retailers and are paid per stop, per route or per day. Profit depends on route density, damage rates and labor costs.

A final mile delivery business handles the last step of the supply chain: taking furniture, appliances, fitness equipment and other bulky goods from a local hub to the customer's door. It is a local, route-based business with steady volume in many markets, but it is very different from running loads off a load board. You manage crews, customer service and damage risk every single day.

How final mile works

Retailers and manufacturers ship goods to a regional warehouse or cross-dock. From there, a final mile carrier picks up a planned route each morning and delivers to homes or businesses within a set area. Service levels vary:

  • Threshold: drop at the door or garage.
  • Room of choice: carry the item inside to the room the customer picks.
  • White glove: carry in, unpack, assemble or install, and remove packaging.
  • Haul-away: take the old item back to the hub or a disposal site.

Higher service levels pay more but take longer per stop and carry more damage risk.

Who you work for

Most final mile carriers do not find their own freight day to day. They usually work through:

  • Logistics companies that hold contracts with big retailers and hire contract carriers to run routes.
  • Retailers or manufacturers directly, often after you build a track record.
  • Regional furniture and appliance stores that need delivery help without running their own trucks.

Some carriers mix final mile with over-the-road box truck freight to fill slow days or weeks. That gives you flexibility but takes planning.

Equipment and crew

Truck

The workhorse of final mile is the non-CDL box truck, commonly around 26 feet with a liftgate. Contracts often set rules on truck age, length, liftgate capacity and appearance. Smaller vans can work for parcels and light items. See box truck vs cargo van for the trade-offs.

Gear checklist

  • Liftgate in good working order
  • Hand trucks and an appliance dolly
  • Moving blankets and pads
  • Straps, load bars and E-track
  • Basic tools for assembly and install
  • Floor protection and shoe covers
  • A phone mount and a reliable delivery app (usually provided by the contractor)

Crew

Bulky goods usually need a two-person team: a driver and a helper. Your crew is the face of the business, since they are in customers' homes. Hire for reliability and attitude, and train on lifting, customer service and damage prevention. Talk to an attorney or CPA about whether crew members should be employees or contractors, because misclassification can be costly.

Paperwork and insurance

Requirements depend on your state, your vehicle weight and whether you cross state lines. Confirm with FMCSA and your state. A typical setup includes:

  1. Business entity and EIN
  2. USDOT number, and operating authority if you haul for-hire across state lines or the contract requires it
  3. Commercial auto liability, cargo insurance and general liability
  4. Workers' compensation if you have employees (rules vary by state)
  5. DOT medical cards and hours-of-service compliance where your vehicle weight requires them

Contractors often require specific insurance limits and name themselves as additional insured. Ask your insurance agent to review the contract's requirements before you sign. Our trucking authority setup service can help with the registration side.

How the money works

Pay models vary by contract: per stop, per route, per day or a combination, sometimes with bonuses or extra pay for assembly and haul-away. The details that hurt carriers most are often in the fine print:

  • Damage chargebacks deducted from settlements
  • Customer complaint penalties or service score requirements
  • Failed delivery pay (or no pay) when the customer is not home
  • Fuel whether it is included or paid separately

Hypothetical daily example

Say a route pays $55 per stop and you run 12 stops.

  • Revenue: 12 x $55 = $660
  • Helper pay for the day: $180
  • Fuel (120 miles at 9 mpg, $3.80/gal): about $51
  • Truck, insurance and maintenance share per day: $220
  • Daily cost before your pay: $451
  • Left for you: $209

One damage chargeback can wipe out that margin. That is why route density, careful handling and a good crew matter so much. Track your true cost with the cost per mile calculator and your crew costs with the driver pay calculator.

Questions to ask before signing a contract

  • How many stops per route on average, and how are routes assigned?
  • How are damage claims investigated and charged back?
  • What happens to pay on failed or refused deliveries?
  • What are the truck, insurance and background check requirements?
  • How often and how fast are settlements paid?
  • Can I run other freight on days with no route?

Get the answers in writing.

Tips to protect your margin

  • Photograph every item at pickup and after delivery, including the packaging. Photos are your best defense against damage claims.
  • Inspect before you load. Note any box damage on the paperwork at the hub, not after the customer opens it.
  • Plan the load order so the last stop goes in first and nothing gets moved twice.
  • Call ahead to confirm the customer is home and to ask about stairs, tight doors or elevators.
  • Keep your crew steady. Training a new helper every month costs time, damage and customer scores.
  • Track results per route so you know which routes make money and which only keep the truck busy.

Frequently asked questions

What truck do I need for final mile delivery?

Many final mile contracts use a 26-foot non-CDL box truck with a liftgate, but some use smaller trucks or cargo vans for parcels. The contract usually spells out the exact truck size, age and features required.

Do final mile carriers need an MC number?

It depends on whether you cross state lines and how the contract is set up. Many contracts ask for a USDOT number and operating authority anyway, so check with FMCSA, your state and the contracting company.

How do final mile carriers get paid?

Common models are per stop, per route, per day or a mix, sometimes with extra pay for assembly or haul-away. Read how damage claims and chargebacks are deducted before you sign.
