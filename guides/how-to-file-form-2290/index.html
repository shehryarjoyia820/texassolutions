How to File Form 2290: Heavy Vehicle Use Tax for Truckers

By Texas Solutions Dispatch Team · October 21, 2026 · 5 min read

Compliance Taxes
Quick answer

How do I file Form 2290 for my truck?

File IRS Form 2290 for each heavy vehicle with a taxable gross weight at or above the IRS threshold, usually by e-filing through an IRS-authorized provider. The return covers a July-to-June tax period and is generally due by the end of the month after the month the truck is first used on public highways. Keep the stamped Schedule 1, because you need it to register or renew plates.

Form 2290 is the IRS return you use to report and pay the federal Heavy Vehicle Use Tax (HVUT) on trucks that run on public highways. If you own a heavy truck in your own name or your company's name, filing it on time is part of keeping your plates and your authority working.

What Form 2290 is and who must file

The HVUT is a yearly federal tax on heavy highway motor vehicles. Form 2290 is how you report each vehicle and pay the tax. In general, you must file if:

  • You own (or are the registered owner of) a highway motor vehicle,
  • Its taxable gross weight is at or above the IRS threshold (historically 55,000 pounds), and
  • It is used on public highways during the tax period.

Taxable gross weight is not the same as the empty weight of the truck. It is roughly the truck's actual unloaded weight, plus the unloaded weight of trailers you usually pull with it, plus the heaviest load you customarily carry. Many owner-operators simply use the weight they register the truck for in their state, but check the Form 2290 instructions for how weight categories work.

The amount of tax depends on the weight category, and the IRS publishes the current rate table in the instructions. We do not quote amounts here because they can change; always use the table for the tax year you are filing.

When Form 2290 is due

The HVUT tax period runs from July 1 to June 30. Two dates matter:

  • Trucks in use in July: the return for the new period is generally due by the last day of August.
  • Trucks first used later in the period: the return is generally due by the last day of the month after the month of first use. For example, a truck first put on the road in October would generally be due by the end of November.

If the due date lands on a weekend or legal holiday, the IRS usually moves it to the next business day. Confirm the exact dates for your situation in the current IRS instructions.

What you need before you file

Gather this before you sit down to file:

  1. EIN for the owner (an SSN is not accepted).
  2. Business name and address exactly as the IRS has them for that EIN.
  3. VIN for every truck you are reporting. A typo here is one of the most common reasons owners must file a correction.
  4. Taxable gross weight category for each truck.
  5. Month of first use in the tax period.
  6. Payment method, such as electronic funds withdrawal or another IRS-approved option.
  7. Whether any truck qualifies as suspended (low mileage).

Step-by-step: how to file Form 2290

  1. Choose how to file. The IRS encourages e-filing, and it is required if you report 25 or more vehicles. Most owner-operators e-file through an IRS-authorized e-file provider. Make sure the provider is listed by the IRS; providers charge their own service fees separate from the tax.
  2. Enter business and vehicle details. Double-check the EIN, name and every VIN.
  3. Select the weight category and month of first use for each truck.
  4. Mark suspended vehicles if a truck is expected to stay under the mileage limit.
  5. Choose a payment option and submit.
  6. Download your stamped Schedule 1. With e-filing, the IRS-watermarked Schedule 1 usually comes back quickly. Paper returns take much longer.
  7. Store copies. Keep the return, Schedule 1 and payment proof with your truck records.

Why the stamped Schedule 1 matters

Schedule 1 is your proof of payment (or proof of suspension). You typically need it to:

  • Register or renew plates, including apportioned plates under IRP.
  • Show proof to a leasing carrier if you run under someone else's authority.
  • Answer questions during an audit or a lease onboarding.

Keep a digital copy on your phone and a paper copy in the office. Many owner-operators also keep one in the truck.

Suspended vehicles and low-mileage trucks

If you expect a truck to be driven no more than the IRS mileage limit during the tax period (5,000 miles in general, with a higher limit for agricultural vehicles), you can report it as suspended. No tax is due, but you still file.

If the truck later goes over the limit, the tax becomes due. The IRS instructions explain the deadline for reporting that change, so mark the date in your calendar if a truck is close.

Worked example: due date for a new truck

Say you buy a truck and first run it on the highway in March.

  • The tax period is the one that started the previous July 1 and ends June 30.
  • Because you first used it in March, the return is generally due by the end of April.
  • The tax for a partial period is prorated by the month of first use, using the IRS table.
  • On July 1 a new tax period starts, and that truck will be due again by the end of August.

That means a truck bought late in the period can need two 2290 filings within a few months. Plan cash flow for both.

Common Form 2290 mistakes to avoid

  • Wrong VIN. Fix it with an amended or corrected filing as the instructions describe.
  • Using an SSN instead of an EIN.
  • Waiting until late August, when e-file systems are busiest and a new EIN may not be active yet.
  • Wrong weight category, especially after changing trailer type or what you haul.
  • Not tracking miles on a suspended truck that ends up working more than expected.
  • Assuming a leasing carrier filed for you. Unless your lease clearly says so in writing, the registered owner is usually responsible.

Track your highway miles carefully, too. The same mileage records help with your fuel tax filings; see our guide to IFTA for owner-operators and the IFTA mileage calculator.

Form 2290 is one of several recurring items for a carrier with its own authority. Others include UCR registration, the MCS-150 biennial update, IFTA returns and IRP renewal. Our guide on how to keep your MC authority active puts these on one calendar, and our trucking back office services page explains the paperwork support we offer.

This guide is general information, not tax advice. Rates, thresholds and deadlines are set by the IRS and can change, so confirm everything in the current Form 2290 instructions at IRS.gov or with a CPA who works with trucking companies.

Frequently asked questions

Can I file Form 2290 with my Social Security number?

No. The IRS requires an Employer Identification Number (EIN) on Form 2290. If you just applied for an EIN, the IRS notes it can take some time before a new EIN is accepted for e-filing, so apply early.

What happens if I file Form 2290 late?

The IRS can charge penalties and interest for late filing and late payment, and you may be unable to register or renew your plates without a stamped Schedule 1. If you are already late, file as soon as possible and ask a tax professional about penalty relief.

Do I file Form 2290 again if I sell my truck?

The tax period does not simply transfer with the truck. Depending on the situation you may be able to claim a credit or refund for a vehicle sold, destroyed or stolen, so keep records and check the current Form 2290 instructions or ask a CPA.

Do I need to file if my truck runs very few miles?

Usually yes. A truck expected to stay under the IRS mileage limit for the period can be reported as suspended, which means no tax is due, but the return itself is still generally required.
